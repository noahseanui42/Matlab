# Linear servo fits leave degrees on the table

A single global linear fit per servo — the model already implemented in `calibrate_servos.m` (`pw = pw_home + sgn*k_us*theta`) — is a defensible *starting point* but not an adequate final calibration for a delta robot arm that needs sub-degree-class end-effector accuracy: the one directly-measured before/after comparison found in this research shows a naive linear formula producing a **3.13° mean positioning error**, falling to **0.19° with a 20-point lookup table** (a roughly 16x reduction on one unit, 2–6x across the two servos tested) ([vishwam-aggarwal/Servo-Calibrator, GitHub](https://github.com/vishwam-aggarwal/Servo-Calibrator)). Because delta-robot end-effector position error scales with the *sine* of small bicep-angle errors times the arm length, a few degrees of per-joint error becomes millimeters of tip error — exactly the accuracy budget a capstone project's protractor-and-camera rig is being built to characterize. The good news is that the fix is cheap: no source in this research recommends jumping to a high-order polynomial for RC servos; the consistently-documented upgrade path is from linear straight to a **multi-point lookup table with interpolation**, built from the same protractor-sweep data already planned, sampled at roughly 7–15 points per servo and swept in both directions to also capture backlash/hysteresis. Below, each of the five questions this project raised — model choice, rig/procedure, prior art, best practices, and pitfalls — is developed in turn, closing with a concrete, numbered plan for the three bicep servos on direct Arduino PWM.

## Why per-servo linear fits are the right skeleton but the wrong final answer

The literature is unanimous that pulse-width-to-angle response is "pretty linear" at a glance but never exactly linear in practice, and — critically — that unit-to-unit variation is large enough that calibrating per physical servo (as `calibrate_servos.m` already does) is mandatory, not optional: two servos of the identical model can show **±100 µs** of variation in the pulse widths that produce the same commanded angle ([Hackster.io / dev.to, Ian Hong](https://dev.to/ihong95/servo-motor-calibration-does-it-matter-1685)). That variation is comparable in scale to the *nonlinearity itself*, which is the real argument for the project's current independent-per-servo design being structurally correct — the mistake would be sharing parameters across servos, not fitting each one separately. Where the current model falls short is curvature: testers of low-cost hobby servos report the commanded-to-actual relationship is not uniform across the sweep, with the low end of travel "stretched," the middle "fairly accurate," and the high end "compressed" ([Arduino Forum](https://forum.arduino.cc/t/solved-servo-calibration-angle-versus-microseconds/603175)) — a shape a single straight line cannot represent no matter how good the endpoint data is. An R² above 0.98 (the threshold `calibrate_servos.m` already warns on) can still hide this: general calibration-curve methodology stresses that R² alone is not sufficient evidence of a good linear fit, and that the residual plot must be inspected for systematic curvature or a fan-shaped (heteroscedastic) pattern before trusting the number ([IntechOpen, Linearity of Calibration Curves for Analytical Methods](https://www.intechopen.com/chapters/58596); [CASRAI Calibration Curve guide](https://casrai.org/guides/analytical-calibration-curve-linear-range-back-calculation)). A servo that is genuinely exceptional — one source names the DS3235 by model as "pretty accurate and linear" enough that a 2-point calibration may suffice ([Hackster.io, Calibrating My Servos](https://www.hackster.io/jeremy-lindsay/calibrating-my-servos-fa27ce)) — is the exception, not a safe default assumption for an unnamed capstone bicep servo.

On the polynomial-versus-lookup-table question specifically, the evidence points one direction: no hobbyist RC-servo project in this research chose a global polynomial fit over a lookup table. The one concrete parametric alternative found, `solosodium/Arduino-PCA9685`'s `ServoProfile` class, fits a **quadratic** curve through exactly three calibration points (0°, 90°, 180°) and is offered as a lightweight middle ground that needs no extra hardware ([solosodium/Arduino-PCA9685, GitHub](https://github.com/solosodium/Arduino-PCA9685)) — but the more heavily validated and more commonly chosen approach, once anyone needs better than "nearest degree" accuracy, is a **direction-averaged lookup table with linear interpolation between points**, exactly as `Servo-Calibrator`'s 20-point table does. Industrial robot calibration literature does use higher-order (e.g., sixth-order Chebyshev) polynomials to jointly model bidirectional positioning error, but explicitly picks the order by minimizing RMSE specifically to avoid overfitting, and even there pairs it with a low-order affine base term rather than a single sweeping high-order fit ([A Modeling and Identification Method for Industrial Robot Positioning Accuracy](https://doi.org/10.3390/machines14060678)). For a three-servo capstone delta arm, a piecewise-linear (interpolated) lookup table built from the sweep data is the right complexity level: it needs no curve-fitting math beyond `interp1`, it naturally represents a flat deadband plus curved end-of-travel region that a low-order polynomial would fight to fit, and it is the documented choice of the one project that actually measured its own improvement.

## Turning the protractor-plus-camera plan into a rig that beats parallax

Every low-cost, buildable rig documented in this research is a variation on "printed protractor, pointed horn, human or camera reads the dial" — this is not a second-tier method invented for this project, it is the dominant documented approach, seen in a 3D-printed protractor dial shared on Thingiverse ([Thingiverse: servo Protractor](https://www.thingiverse.com/thing:4315300)), in Jeremy Lindsay's rig built specifically to find "what PWM values turn servos to a specific angle" ([Hackster.io, Calibrating My Servos](https://www.hackster.io/jeremy-lindsay/calibrating-my-servos-fa27ce)), in Ian Hong's notched jig sampled every 15° ([Hackster.io, Exploring Servo Motor Calibration](https://www.hackster.io/ian-hong/exploring-servo-motor-calibration-414b95)), and in a University of Washington CSE466 student project using the identical notch-jig approach ([UW CSE466 ServoCar calibration writeup](https://courses.cs.washington.edu/courses/cse466/01au/Projects/ServoCar/maxx/building/calibrate.pdf)). The consistently-named failure mode across these write-ups is **parallax** — misreading where the pointer crosses the dial because the viewing/camera axis is not exactly perpendicular to the protractor plane — and the consistently-suggested fix is not a better photograph but a sensor that removes the human read entirely ([search synthesis re: Hackster.io protractor calibration](https://www.hackster.io/jeremy-lindsay/calibrating-my-servos-fa27ce)). General measurement-science guidance backs the same physical cause: to reduce parallax on an analog scale, view it exactly perpendicular and as close to the graduation plane as possible ([How to Reduce Parallax Error](https://answers.completeera.com/post/how-to-reduce-parallax-error-precision-tips-for-measurements)). Since this project has already committed to protractor+pointer+camera rather than an encoder, the concrete design levers are: mount the camera on a **fixed tripod/jig perpendicular to the protractor face** (not handheld), center the protractor's pivot on the camera's optical axis, sand or print the pointer tip to a fine point rather than a flat paddle (a 1–2 mm wide printed line at a small pointer radius already subtends 1–2° of arc, which is a meaningful fraction of the error budget), and use a rigid, non-flexing horn extension — printed-jig write-ups implicitly assume this but none quantify how much horn-arm flex itself contributes error. No source in this research gives a hard number for camera-based protractor accuracy (this is a genuine, flagged gap across every write-up found), so the project should not assume the rig is better than "nearest degree" until it validates that empirically — see the validation approach below.

Because no purpose-built tool exists for turning a photo of a protractor+pointer into an angle automatically, the closest transferable code is a generic OpenCV analog-gauge-reader that detects the needle as a line and computes its angle via arctangent (`dgomes/analog-gauge-reader`, Python + OpenCV) ([dgomes/analog-gauge-reader, GitHub](https://github.com/dgomes/analog-gauge-reader)); its own documented caveats — good, shadow-free lighting is essential, and thin needles perform worse than thick ones — translate directly to a servo-pointer rig and argue for a thick, high-contrast printed pointer under diffuse lighting rather than a thin arrow. For comparison, the one alternative rig this research quantified numerically — an AS5600 magnetic encoder glued to the output shaft, read continuously over I²C — is worth naming even though the project has already chosen protractor+camera: it costs roughly **$10–15**, needs only wiring plus a handful of Arduino libraries, and is the only rig in this research with a measured accuracy number (3.13°→0.19°) ([vishwam-aggarwal/Servo-Calibrator, GitHub](https://github.com/vishwam-aggarwal/Servo-Calibrator)). If the protractor+camera rig's own parallax-limited accuracy turns out (during validation) to be worse than roughly a degree, an AS5600 add-on per servo is a low-cost fallback that could later replace or cross-check the camera reads without redesigning the whole calibration procedure. A third alternative, AprilTag/ArUco fiducial tracking, is real prior art (used to track a hexapod's joints via a fixed overhead camera) but the one documented implementation reports only a **5° repeatability floor** because it used a simplified single-plane camera calibration rather than full multi-pose intrinsics/distortion calibration ([lukas/hexapod-tracker, GitHub](https://github.com/lukas/hexapod-tracker)) — worse than the encoder option and not clearly better than a well-executed protractor read, so it is not recommended as a replacement for the chosen rig.

## Delta firmware calibration math is reusable; servo-specific delta calibration code is not

Nothing in the open-source ecosystem calibrates a servo-driven delta robot's PWM-to-angle curves end-to-end — that combination (servo actuation, delta-arm geometry, camera-based angle ground truth) does not appear to exist as a published project. What does exist, richly documented, is the **geometric calibration philosophy** used by every mature delta 3D-printer firmware: Marlin's `G33` auto-calibration probes a grid of points and solves for delta radius, arm length, and per-tower angular offsets ([Marlin G33 docs](https://marlinfw.org/docs/gcode/G033.html)), RepRapFirmware/Duet3D exposes an explicit **3/4/6/8-factor** family of increasingly complex calibration models (endstop-only, +radius, +tower position, +tower tilt) ([Duet3D Delta calibration docs](https://docs.duet3d.com/User_manual/Tuning/Delta_calibration)), and Klipper's calibration solves for tower endstops, tower angles, delta radius, and arm length from as few as seven probe points ([Klipper Delta_Calibrate.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Klipper3d/klipper/master/docs/Delta_Calibrate.md)). None of these calibrate a *servo's* pulse-width curve — they calibrate stepper-driven geometric parameters against a touch probe — but the architecture (parameterize the systematic error, collect measurements spanning the workspace, solve the parameter vector by least squares, and scale complexity in stages) is a template this project could reuse *above* the per-servo PWM fit: once each bicep servo has its own angle-to-pulse table, a second, whole-arm calibration pass (measuring end-effector position at several points with the same camera rig) could solve for residual geometric parameters — arm-length errors, tower-angle offsets, home-position skew — the same way Duet3D's staged factor models do. Academic delta-robot calibration work confirms this two-level approach pays off in the same units this project cares about: one study reports absolute accuracy improving from **~1.4 mm to ~0.1–0.13 mm** after full kinematic calibration ([Cambridge Core / ResearchGate, Kinematic calibration of the parallel Delta robot](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/robotica/article/abs/kinematic-calibration-of-the-parallel-delta-robot/A72E015D75BFF1D8EBE2A503B7441405)), and a related paper specifically addresses delta robots whose three arms are **not perfectly symmetric** ([ScienceDirect, Kinematic sensitivity of a non-fully symmetric parallel Delta robot](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0094114X21000690)) — directly relevant to a hand-built capstone arm where the three bicep linkages are very unlikely to be identical.

At the servo-code level, two directly reusable references exist beyond the stock `Servo.h` linear `attach(pin, min, max)` pattern: `solosodium/Arduino-PCA9685`'s `ServoProfile` class, which stores three reference points and fits a quadratic, plus an interactive serial calibration sketch (`set;<angle>;<pulse_ms>`, `test;<angle>`) that is directly adaptable to a non-PCA9685, direct-PWM Arduino sketch since the profile math is independent of the PWM-generation backend ([solosodium/Arduino-PCA9685, GitHub](https://github.com/solosodium/Arduino-PCA9685)); and `vishwam-aggarwal/Servo-Calibrator`, whose firmware state machine (coarse-then-fine bidirectional sweep, averaging two directions into a table, exporting a ready-to-compile `CalPoint` array) is the most complete worked example of *how* to structure a sweep-and-build-table routine, even though its ground truth is an encoder rather than a camera ([vishwam-aggarwal/Servo-Calibrator, GitHub](https://github.com/vishwam-aggarwal/Servo-Calibrator)). On the MATLAB/curve-fitting side, there is no packaged library to reach for — every source converges on a short custom `polyfit` (or, for a table, `interp1`) script against manually recorded (pulse width, angle) pairs, which is exactly what `calibrate_servos.m` already is ([Swarthmore E5 Matlab servo lab](https://archive.swarthmore.edu/www1/NatSci/ceverba1/Class/e5/LabServo/E5Matlab3.html); [SciPy curve_fit docs](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.optimize.curve_fit.html) for the Python-side equivalent). This confirms the project's existing script is aligned with actual practice — extending it to a table-based fit is an incremental change, not a rewrite, and the project would not be reinventing something that already exists off-the-shelf.

## Sweep design: point count, hysteresis, repeats, and a validation threshold

General calibration-curve statistics (analytical chemistry and metrology, not servo-specific, but directly transferable to fitting any actuator response) treat **roughly 5–6 evenly-spaced points as a practical floor** for a defensible fit and flag that more points mainly help detect curvature rather than fundamentally change a well-behaved fit ([CASRAI Calibration Curve guide](https://casrai.org/guides/analytical-calibration-curve-linear-range-back-calculation)). Servo-specific practice sits on both sides of that floor: some hobbyists use as few as 2–3 anchor points (min, center, max) when they already trust linearity, while others sample every 10–15° specifically to check for it, implying roughly **12–13 points across a 180° range** ([dev.to, Ian Hong](https://dev.to/ihong95/servo-motor-calibration-does-it-matter-1685)). Synthesizing the two, **7–15 points evenly spaced across the servo's usable range**, with 2–3 additional points held out purely for validation (never used in the fit), is a reasonable target for a table-based per-servo calibration — enough to support linear interpolation between neighboring points without needing a curve-fit at all, and enough residual degrees of freedom to catch a mis-recorded point. No source recommends deliberately denser sampling near the mechanical limits, but this remains a sound inference given the same sources' repeated observation that hobby-servo response is most "stretched" or "compressed" precisely at the range extremes ([Arduino Forum](https://forum.arduino.cc/t/solved-servo-calibration-angle-versus-microseconds/603175)).

Sweeping in one direction only will silently bake a servo's direction-dependent backlash into the fitted curve; the accepted fix, seen in both a patented industrial "transmission hysteresis detecting method" ([USPTO patent 10727821](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10727821)) and in `Servo-Calibrator`'s firmware, is to **sweep the full range in both directions and either average the two into one table or fit them separately and report the gap as the backlash figure** ([vishwam-aggarwal/Servo-Calibrator, GitHub](https://github.com/vishwam-aggarwal/Servo-Calibrator)). No source gives an explicit "flag if forward/backward disagree by more than X degrees" rule, so this project should set its own threshold from its own accuracy target (see the recommendations table below) rather than expect one from the literature. On repeat trials, industrial and metrology sources confirm repeat-and-average is standard practice but do not converge on a specific count, framing repeatability as a spread (standard deviation) to be characterized rather than a fixed number of samples ([search synthesis, dspowerservo.com](https://www.dspowerservo.com/news/why-is-the-servo-motor-angle-accuracy-so-important/)); a student project with limited bench time should treat roughly **3–5 repeats per point** as enough to compute a meaningful mean and spread without making the sweep impractically long, and should record that spread, not just the mean, at each point.

Validation should not stop at R². General calibration methodology recommends checking the residual pattern for systematic curvature or a fan-shaped (heteroscedastic) spread, using formal lack-of-fit tests where justified, and reserving a held-out test set that plays no role in fitting the model ([IntechOpen, Linearity of Calibration Curves](https://www.intechopen.com/chapters/58596); [scikit-learn cross-validation docs](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/cross_validation.html)). A commonly cited analytical-chemistry rule of thumb is **R² ≥ 0.995** for an acceptable linear calibration ([CASRAI Calibration Curve guide](https://casrai.org/guides/analytical-calibration-curve-linear-range-back-calculation)), though servos are noisier instruments than lab analytes, so relaxing to roughly **0.99** and pairing it with a residual check is more realistic; `calibrate_servos.m`'s existing 0.98 warning threshold is in the right neighborhood but should be treated as a smoke alarm, not proof of fit quality, precisely because a curved response can still post a high R². Drift with temperature, voltage, and load is a real mechanism — a potentiometer's resistance changes with temperature and can be read by the servo's own control loop as a spurious position change ([general potentiometer drift literature](https://www.analog.com/en/resources/app-notes/an-1121.html)), and supply-voltage sag measurably changes torque/speed (one servo's spec moves from 3.3 kg·cm at 4.8 V to 4.1 kg·cm at 6 V) in a way that plausibly degrades positional accuracy under load or a sagging battery ([servo voltage/torque discussion](https://microservomotor.com/common-specifications-and-parameters/micro-servo-speed-accuracy.htm)) — but no source in this research gives a servo-specific number in µs/°C or µs/V, so this remains a mechanism to guard against procedurally (calibrate at the actual operating voltage and re-check after the rig has warmed up) rather than a correction the project can look up and apply directly.

## Backlash, brownout resets, and a non-repeatable zero are the real error floor

The pitfall most likely to dominate this project's error budget is backlash, and it is worth sizing concretely: plastic-gear hobby servos (SG90-class) show roughly **1–2°** of direction-dependent return-to-center error, while metal-gear equivalents (MG90S) show roughly **0.5°** ([Zbotic, MG90S vs SG90](https://zbotic.in/mg90s-vs-sg90-torque-accuracy-best-uses-compared/)), and this is separate from — and can be confused with — spline/horn-mounting play, which shifts the effective zero independently of the gear train itself ([RCTech forum, Servo Horn Spline Play](https://www.rctech.net/forum/radio-electronics/588982-servo-horn-has-little-play-spline.html)). Within the backlash dead zone, finer pulse-width steps produce no angular change at all ("having increased resolution in the backlash zone is of no value as the gear output does not move," [RoboticsTomorrow](https://www.roboticstomorrow.com/article/2023/10/what-are-resolution-accuracy-repeatability/21329)), and backlash is easy to misdiagnose as linkage flex or control-loop lag unless the rig specifically tests both approach directions ([GES Repair, Backlash Isn't Always Mechanical](https://gesrepair.com/backlash-in-servo-systems-isnt-always-mechanical)) — reinforcing why the bidirectional sweep above is not optional for this project. The servo's "zero" is likewise not a fixed, trustworthy constant: potentiometer resistance tolerance (commonly ±10–20% in low-cost servos), wiper-mounting tolerance, and comparator reference-voltage variance mean two nominally identical servos — or the same servo after wear — will not agree on what pulse width means "center" ([Pololu blog, Servo Control Interface in Detail](https://www.pololu.com/blog/17/servo-control-interface-in-detail)), which is the underlying justification for calibrating every unit independently rather than trusting a factory nominal 1500 µs.

On the electronics side, direct-Arduino-PWM drive (no PCA9685) has two specific, well-documented failure modes worth guarding against during a sweep. First, powering servos from the Arduino's own 5V rail risks brownout resets: a servo's motor draws a **2–3x current spike for 10–50 ms** on every large or reversing move, which is exactly what a calibration sweep repeatedly does, and the recommended fix is a **470–1000 µF capacitor** across the servo supply, placed close to the servo, with a shared ground ([Industrial Monitor Direct, Powering SG90 from Arduino 5V Rail](https://industrialmonitordirect.com/blogs/knowledgebase/powering-sg90-servo-from-arduino-direct-5v-rail-design-guide)) — a brownout mid-sweep would silently restart the calibration routine and could corrupt or truncate a recorded dataset without an obvious symptom. Second, the Arduino `Servo` library's software-timer-based pulse generation is subject to jitter under multi-servo contention (reported up to **~77 µs** when several PWM channels compete for the same timer) ([QEC, Arduino vs 86Duino PWM Jitter](https://www.qec.tw/86duino-library/servo/comparison-of-arduino-and-86duino-servo-library-pwm-jitter-phenomena/)) and its real resolution is board-dependent — 1 µs steps on classic AVR (Uno/Mega) cores but as coarse as **4 µs** on a Nano Every ([Arduino Forum, Limited writeMicroseconds Resolution](https://forum.arduino.cc/t/limited-writemicroseconds-resolution-when-using-servo-library/657592)). At roughly **5.56 µs per degree** for a 1000 µs-span, 180° servo ([servo PWM/timer resolution discussion](https://joe.blog.freemansoft.com/2023/01/servo-pulse-width-modulation-pwm-timer.html)), 1 µs resolution is about 0.18°/step and 4 µs is about 0.7°/step — both smaller than the 0.5–2° backlash figures above on a classic AVR board, confirming that **mechanical backlash, not microsecond quantization, is the practical accuracy ceiling** for this project's hardware class, so long as the calibration code is verified to run at 1 µs resolution on whatever board is actually driving the servos. Finally, because a bare hobby servo has no independent position feedback, any "read-back" of angle from the library itself is just the last commanded value, not a measurement — genuine validation requires the external protractor/camera reference this project has already planned, and settling time (roughly 2 seconds is a commonly used conservative default in example code) must be respected before a photo is taken, or overshoot will be recorded as position error.

## Concrete plan for the three bicep servos

The table below turns the research above into specific, numbered steps sized to this project: three bicep servos on direct Arduino PWM, pulse-width range roughly 830–2170 µs, calibrated with a protractor + fixed pointer + camera rig.

| Step | Recommendation | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Model | Upgrade `calibrate_servos.m` from a single `polyfit(theta, pw, 1)` to a **direction-averaged lookup table with linear interpolation** (`interp1` at runtime), keeping the linear fit only as a fallback/sanity check and for extrapolation warnings | The only measured comparison found shows a table cutting error from 3.13° to 0.19° vs. a linear formula ([Servo-Calibrator](https://github.com/vishwam-aggarwal/Servo-Calibrator)); a table needs no new math beyond what the project's CSV-based workflow already does |
| 2. Sweep points | **10–12 points per servo**, evenly spaced across the usable travel implied by 830–2170 µs, plus 2–3 held-out points not used in the table, for validation only | Splits the difference between the general 5–6 point statistical floor and servo-specific practice of ~12–15 points to catch curvature ([CASRAI](https://casrai.org/guides/analytical-calibration-curve-linear-range-back-calculation); [dev.to](https://dev.to/ihong95/servo-motor-calibration-does-it-matter-1685)) |
| 3. Direction | Sweep **forward (increasing pulse width) and backward (decreasing) separately** at every point, log both, and either average them into the table or report the forward/backward gap as a per-servo backlash number | Removes direction-dependent backlash/hysteresis from contaminating the fit ([USPTO patent 10727821](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10727821); [Servo-Calibrator](https://github.com/vishwam-aggarwal/Servo-Calibrator)) |
| 4. Repeats | **3–5 repeat photos per point per direction**; record mean and standard deviation, not just the mean | Repeat-and-average is standard practice ([dspowerservo.com](https://www.dspowerservo.com/news/why-is-the-servo-motor-angle-accuracy-so-important/)); the spread itself tells you if the camera read is the noise floor or the servo is |
| 5. Rig | Mount the camera on a fixed jig, **perpendicular to the protractor face, pivot centered on the optical axis**; use a thick, high-contrast printed pointer, sanded to a fine tip; diffuse, shadow-free lighting | Parallax and thin-pointer misreads are the named dominant error sources in every protractor write-up found ([search synthesis](https://www.hackster.io/jeremy-lindsay/calibrating-my-servos-fa27ce); [analog-gauge-reader caveats](https://github.com/dgomes/analog-gauge-reader)) |
| 6. Settle time | Wait for mechanical settling (start conservative, ~1–2 s, then tighten once you've watched a step response) before capturing each photo | Reading before settling records overshoot as calibration error, not servo error |
| 7. Power | Add a **470–1000 µF capacitor** across the servo supply rail near each servo, and confirm shared ground with the Arduino, before running any sweep | Sweeps repeatedly trigger the 2–3x current spikes that cause brownout resets ([Industrial Monitor Direct](https://industrialmonitordirect.com/blogs/knowledgebase/powering-sg90-servo-from-arduino-direct-5v-rail-design-guide)) |
| 8. Firmware resolution | Verify (don't assume) the microcontroller's actual `writeMicroseconds()` resolution is 1 µs, not 4 µs, on the specific board used | Board-dependent resolution differences (Uno vs. Nano Every) change whether quantization or backlash dominates the error budget ([Arduino Forum](https://forum.arduino.cc/t/limited-writemicroseconds-resolution-when-using-servo-library/657592)) |
| 9. Fit warning threshold | Keep an R² warning but lower confidence in it alone; additionally plot residuals and flag any point whose residual exceeds ~1–2° (or check forward/backward disagreement) rather than trusting R² ≥ 0.98 in isolation | R² can stay high even with visible curvature or heteroscedastic noise ([IntechOpen](https://www.intechopen.com/chapters/58596)) |
| 10. Validation | After building the table, command the **held-out points** (never used in the fit) and confirm the camera-measured angle is within your target tolerance (start at ±1–2°, tighten if the rig proves better) | Held-out validation is the standard defense against overfitting a small dataset ([scikit-learn cross-validation](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/cross_validation.html)) |
| 11. Whole-arm follow-up (stretch goal) | Once all three servos have individual tables, run a second calibration pass measuring end-effector position at several points with the same camera, and solve for residual geometric parameters (arm length, tower offsets) the way Duet3D's staged factor models do | Per-servo PWM calibration alone does not correct mechanical/geometric assembly errors; delta-specific literature shows this second pass is where accuracy gains from ~1.4 mm to ~0.1 mm ([Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/robotica/article/abs/kinematic-calibration-of-the-parallel-delta-robot/A72E015D75BFF1D8EBE2A503B7441405)) |

## Conclusion

The clearest takeaway is that this project's instinct to calibrate each servo independently was already correct — unit-to-unit variation is too large to share parameters — but its choice of *model* (single linear fit) is the one place the literature consistently says to go further, and the documented next step is a direction-averaged lookup table, not a higher-order polynomial. The second-order insight is that the protractor+camera rig, while the right low-cost choice, inherits a specific, named weakness (parallax and pointer-width error) that no source has actually quantified for this exact setup; the project should treat its own held-out-point validation step as the first real measurement of how good "protractor + camera" is, rather than assuming it matches either the "nearest degree" folklore or the 0.19° encoder benchmark. Finally, because no one has published a servo-actuated delta-robot calibration pipeline, this capstone sits in a genuine gap between two well-documented worlds — RC-servo PWM calibration and delta-printer geometric calibration — and bridging them (per-servo tables feeding into a second whole-arm geometric calibration pass, borrowed from Marlin/Klipper/RepRapFirmware's staged factor-model philosophy) is not just an implementation detail but a small, legitimate original contribution worth stating explicitly in the report.
