# Servo PWM-to-Angle Calibration Sweep and Fit Validation: Practical Best Practices

## How many angle/pulse-width points do practitioners typically sweep, and how are they spaced (evenly in angle, evenly in pulse width, denser near limits)?

### Takeaway
Servo-specific practitioner writeups (hobbyist and course-level) most often use a *sparse* set of anchor points — commonly just the two mechanical extremes plus center (3 points) or a handful more — because the pulse-width-to-angle relationship is treated as linear/near-linear; general analytical-calibration best practice, which is not servo-specific but transfers directly to the statistics of the fit, calls for at least 5–6 evenly-spaced levels spanning the full range to reliably estimate slope/intercept and detect curvature.

### Cited Findings
- Standard hobby servo PWM range is ~1.0–2.0 ms (or 544–2400 µs on some servos) mapped to 0–180°, with the neutral/center position at ~1.5 ms; the mapping is treated as linear across the calibrated envelope (`pulse_us = min_us + (angle/180)*(max_us-min_us)`) — [DroneBot Workshop / general PWM servo overview](https://dronebotworkshop.com/servo-motors-with-arduino/), [Project Gus – Servo pulse width range with Arduino](https://www.projectgus.com/2009/07/servo-pulse-width-range-with-arduino/)
- A common hobbyist calibration workflow explicitly tests `writeMicroseconds()` at the extremes to find the servo's *actual* 0° and 180° positions before building anything mechanical, i.e., a 2–3 point calibration (min, max, and a center check) — [search synthesis citing Arduino/servo calibration guides](https://industrialmonitordirect.com/blogs/knowledgebase/rc-servo-pwm-pulse-width-calibration-safe-timing-limits)
- Ian Hong's servo calibration writeup states the pulse-width/angle relationship "looks pretty linear," so a line of best fit from a modest number of measured points is sufficient to calculate pulse width for any angle; he separately notes that if the relationship were perfectly linear, the pulse-width difference between any two 15°-spaced increments would be identical — implying his practical sweep used roughly 15° increments across the range (i.e., ~12–13 points across 180°) to check for that linearity — [Servo Motor Calibration: Does It Matter? (dev.to)](https://dev.to/ihong95/servo-motor-calibration-does-it-matter-1685), [Exploring Servo Motor Calibration (Hackster.io)](https://www.hackster.io/ian-hong/exploring-servo-motor-calibration-414b95)
- Jeremy Lindsay's "Calibrating My Servos" project built a physical rig with a protractor to measure actual output angle at commanded PWM values, targeting specific reference angles (e.g., 0° and 90°) rather than a dense sweep — [Calibrating My Servos (Hackster.io)](https://www.hackster.io/jeremy-lindsay/calibrating-my-servos-fa27ce)
- A university embedded-systems course (Iowa State CprE 288) teaches servo calibration explicitly because "the servo does not always sweep 180 degrees" when commanded with library defaults, and has students determine correction points against the scan/servo library defaults — [CprE 288 Servo Calibration Reference Sheet](http://class.ece.iastate.edu/cpre288/resources/programs/Servo-Calibration-Reference-Sheet.pdf) (title/description only; full PDF text not retrievable in this session — see Gaps)
- General analytical-chemistry calibration-curve guidance (not servo-specific, but the statistical logic transfers directly to any linear sensor/actuator calibration): "at least five non-zero concentrations is a practical working minimum for routine analytical work, with regulated bioanalytical method validation commonly calling for a blank plus at least six non-zero levels. More points, evenly spaced across the range, improve the reliability of the fitted slope and intercept and make curvature easier to spot." — [CASRAI: Calibration Curve guide](https://casrai.org/guides/analytical-calibration-curve-linear-range-back-calculation)
- General metrology/statistics guidance also stresses that points should be spread across the *entire* intended operating range (not clustered), because extrapolation beyond the calibrated span is unreliable, and that inspecting the raw calibration plot for outliers/curvature/non-linear trends should be the first step before any fit is trusted — [Linearity of Calibration Curves for Analytical Methods (IntechOpen review)](https://www.intechopen.com/chapters/58596)

### Inferences
- For a servo whose native pulse-width-to-angle behavior is close to linear (most analog/digital hobby RC servos), practitioners appear to converge on a small number of *anchor* points (2–3: min, max, center) for a quick calibration, but a denser sweep (~10–15 points, e.g. every 10–15°) is used specifically to *verify* linearity and catch deviations near the mechanical limits, where most hobby servos are known to become non-linear or stall against end-stops.
- Because general calibration-curve statistics (from analytical chemistry/metrology, not servo-specific) recommend 5+ evenly spaced levels as a floor for reliable slope/intercept estimation and curvature detection, a servo sweep with only 2–3 points is defensible only if you already trust the linear model and only need to fix offset/scale, not verify shape.
- No source found explicitly recommends non-uniform (denser-near-limits) spacing for servo PWM calibration specifically; the "denser near limits" idea is plausible because servo non-linearity/stalling concentrates near the mechanical stops, but this is an inference, not a directly cited recommendation.

## Do people commonly sweep forward and backward separately to detect hysteresis/backlash, and how do they combine or report the two directions?

### Takeaway
Yes — forward/reverse (increasing/decreasing command) sweeps are a standard technique for detecting hysteresis and backlash in geared/servo systems, both in patented industrial testing methods and in general servo/robotics practice, but no source found gives a single universally-cited combination rule (average vs. fit separately) or an explicit disagreement threshold in degrees; this appears to be handled case-by-case.

### Cited Findings
- A "transmission hysteresis detecting method" (patent) explicitly transmits forward and reverse rotational instructions to a motor, records the motor and output-shaft end positions for both forward and reverse cycles, and calculates transmission hysteresis by comparing the two cycles — [Transmission hysteresis detecting method and apparatus (patent)](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10727821)
- General servo/robotics hysteresis-testing guidance describes stepping a command from 40% to 60% of range specifically to "capture slack and reduce inaccuracy in parameter estimation" — i.e., a targeted small-range forward/backward step rather than a full sweep, used to isolate backlash — [search synthesis of servo hysteresis testing methodology sources, incl. Mr. D "Servo Testing Methodology"] (page content not independently fetchable in this session; treat as a single aggregated finding, see Gaps)
- Backlash/hysteresis in RC servos is described as having both a mechanical-linkage component and an electronic "deadband" component in the servo's own control logic (it stops correcting once "close enough" to target), meaning a forward/backward sweep captures the combined effect of both, not pure mechanical backlash alone — [Parallax Forums discussion on servo direction-dependent stopping point (title/topic only, not fetchable) and general hysteresis literature synthesis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2214785320372680)
- A hobby-servo forum thread is specifically titled around a servo "stopping in a different place depending on travel direction," indicating this is a commonly observed and discussed phenomenon among practitioners, though the thread's content (specific numeric magnitude, resolution approach) could not be retrieved in this session — [Parallax Forums: Servo stops in different place depending on travel direction](https://forums.parallax.com/discussion/96609/servo-stops-in-different-place-depending-on-travel-direction)
- Independent, non-servo-specific transmission/backlash literature confirms that geared mechanical systems in a quasi-static state show "significant hysteresis" between commanded input and actual output — a general mechanical-systems fact that supports why forward/backward testing is needed for any geared actuator, servos included — [Inferential based measurement of backlash in servo system (ScienceDirect)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2214785320372680)
- Robotic testing practice for repeatability (adjacent to hysteresis testing) involves commanding a servo to specific target angles (e.g., 0°, 90°, 180°) from both directions of approach, measuring actual position, and computing deviation, with results then averaged over repeated trials — [search synthesis on servo repeatability testing at multiple target angles](https://www.dspowerservo.com/news/why-is-the-servo-motor-angle-accuracy-so-important/)

### Inferences
- Given that hysteresis is explicitly measured as a *difference* between forward and reverse end positions (per the patent methodology), the practical default for a calibration fit is likely to either (a) fit the forward and backward sweeps as two separate linear models and report the offset between them as the "backlash," or (b) average the two directions into one fit when the offset is small relative to desired accuracy, and flag/report when it is not. No source gives a specific degree threshold for "small enough to average" — this is a gap.
- The 40–60%-of-range step technique for isolating backlash suggests that a full 0–180° two-direction sweep is not strictly necessary to *detect* hysteresis; a smaller-amplitude direction-reversal test near the operating region of interest can suffice, which may be more practical for a time-limited student project.

### Gaps
- No source gave a concrete "flag if forward/backward sweeps disagree by more than X degrees" rule; this number was not found in the literature reviewed and would need to be set by the practitioner based on their own accuracy requirements (see the R²/residual "good enough" numbers under the validation question below, which could reasonably be reused as the disagreement threshold).
- Full text of the Mr. D "Servo Testing Methodology" page, the Parallax Forums thread, and the ScienceDirect backlash-measurement paper could not be retrieved (WebFetch blocked for these domains in this session); only search-engine-synthesized summaries were available. Direct retrieval by a human researcher is recommended to confirm specifics and pull any numeric backlash values reported.

## How many repeat trials per point do people typically take to characterize measurement noise/repeatability, and what's reasonable for a student project?

### Takeaway
Practitioner and industrial sources describe repeating measurements at each target angle and averaging to reduce noise, but no source gave a single canonical trial count; industrial/patent methodology implies "a predetermined number of repetitions" (unspecified, tunable), while general encoder-accuracy literature frames repeatability as a statistical spread (standard deviation) rather than a fixed trial count — for a student project, repeating each point roughly 3–10 times (enough to compute a meaningful mean and standard deviation without excessive time cost) is a reasonable inference, not a directly cited number.

### Cited Findings
- A described repeatability-evaluation procedure: "rotate the servo at multiple target angles (such as 0°, 90°, and 180°), measure the actual position and calculate the deviation, then repeat this process multiple times and take the average" — confirms repeated trials + averaging is standard practice, without specifying an exact count — [search synthesis, servo angle accuracy discussion](https://www.dspowerservo.com/news/why-is-the-servo-motor-angle-accuracy-so-important/)
- An industrial calibration/velocity-measurement patent describes an algorithm that repeats measurement until a "predetermined number of repetition times" is reached, then averages — confirming that repeat-and-average is an established calibration technique, though the specific count is implementation-defined and not disclosed in the summary retrieved — [Servo controlled calibration of repeatable errors in position transducers (patent)](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6683461)
- Encoder/position-sensor metrology (general precision-measurement practice, not servo-specific but directly transferable) frames repeatability as "the ability to consistently make the same measurement and get the same result," typically 2–10× better (smaller spread) than absolute accuracy for a given encoder — implying that repeatability characterization is really about capturing a *distribution* (mean + spread) at each point rather than hitting a specific trial-count target — [Understanding Resolution, Accuracy and Repeatability, Celera Motion TN-1005](https://www.celeramotion.com/optical-sensors/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2019/05/TN-1005_Resolution_Accuracy_-and_Repeatability.pdf) (title/summary only; full PDF not fetchable in this session)
- One reported practical measurement: mean absolute position deviation of about 2 encoder steps, or 0.17°, was measured for a servo/encoder combination under test — an example of the kind of repeatability figure obtainable, though this is for one specific hardware setup, not a general servo constant — [search synthesis referencing encoder/servo repeatability testing]

### Inferences
- Since no source specifies an exact "good" trial count, and general measurement-uncertainty practice (not servo-specific) treats 3 repeats as a bare minimum to get any sense of spread and ~10+ as more statistically defensible for a symmetric confidence interval, a student project with limited time should reasonably target on the order of 5 repeats per point as a practical compromise — this is an inference from general measurement-statistics norms, not a servo-specific citation.
- Because repeatability is fundamentally about characterizing spread (not just averaging away noise), a student report should record and report the standard deviation (or range) of repeated trials at each point, not just the mean — consistent with the general encoder-metrology framing above.

### Gaps
- No servo-specific source gave an explicit "N repeats per point" recommendation; the CMU-RI-TR-09-41 (Tira-Thompson) "Digital Servo Calibration and Modeling" report was identified via search as likely to contain a concrete methodology (it explicitly aims to avoid "precision rigs or expensive measurement devices" to stay accessible to "classrooms, laboratories, and garages" — exactly the student-project use case) but could not be fetched in this session due to WebFetch being blocked for ri.cmu.edu and tekkotsu.org. This is the single most promising unretrieved source for this key question and should be pursued directly — [CMU-RI-TR-09-41 PDF, ri.cmu.edu](https://www.ri.cmu.edu/pub_files/2009/3/CMU-RI-TR-09-41.pdf), [mirror at tekkotsu.org](http://www.tekkotsu.org/media/dynamixel_study_by_ejt.pdf)

## How much does servo response (deadband, center point, slope) drift with temperature, supply voltage, or load, and is recalibration/compensation commonly recommended? Any concrete numbers?

### Takeaway
Servo-specific quantitative drift numbers (µs/°C, µs/V, degrees/kg-load) were not found in the sources retrieved; what is well documented is the *mechanism* — potentiometer resistance temperature-coefficient causing apparent position drift, voltage sag reducing torque/speed (and by extension positional accuracy under load), and general recommendations that positional drift be checked periodically (e.g., re-verifying calibrated endpoints after 24 hours) — but hard numbers are mostly available only for related but non-identical general-electronics components (e.g., digital potentiometer TempCo), not off-the-shelf hobby RC servos.

### Cited Findings
- **Temperature (mechanism, general electronics — not servo-specific):** a potentiometer's resistive element value changes proportionally with temperature ("TempCo"), and in a high-temperature environment the potentiometer may show a drop in resistance that the electronics interpret as a change in position — causing the controller to command corrective (spurious) movement — [general potentiometer/position-sensor drift literature](https://www.analog.com/en/resources/app-notes/an-1121.html), [Temperature compensation circuit and method for position sensors (patent)](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/4628316)
- **Temperature (concrete number, general electronics, not servo-specific):** a digital potentiometer product can achieve as low as 5 ppm/°C ratiometric temperature coefficient for low-drift applications — given as a *general electronics reference point* for how small a "good" TempCo can be, not a measured value for an RC servo's internal pot — [Analog Devices AN-1121 / DS1845-DS1855 TempCo analysis](https://www.analog.com/en/resources/technical-articles/ds1845ds1855-temperature-coefficient-analysis.html)
- **Voltage (servo-specific, with concrete numbers):** one servo's rated performance changes from 0.21 s/60° at 3.3 kg·cm torque at 4.8 V to 0.16 s/60° at 4.1 kg·cm torque at 6 V — a direct, concrete illustration that supply voltage measurably changes torque and speed; the same source notes that voltage *sag* under load (e.g., system dips to 5.5 V under load even though rated at 6 V) means the servo will not achieve its rated 6V performance in practice, degrading positional accuracy under load because the servo can no longer maintain commanded torque/speed and may stall or lose position — [servo voltage sag / torque-speed tradeoff discussion](https://microservomotor.com/common-specifications-and-parameters/micro-servo-speed-accuracy.htm), [related torque-control guide](https://picosystems.store/feeds/blog/torque-control-servo-motor)
- **Load (general, servo/robot-arm specific):** joint torque needed to hold or move a robot arm against gravity increases with how far the load is extended from the joint (moment arm), and total required torque = gravity component (from links + payload) + acceleration component; when a load-bearing joint is at a position with less mechanical advantage, insufficient available torque directly causes position/tracking error (the servo cannot fully counteract the load-induced torque) — [How to Determine What Torque You Need for Your Servo Motors, Automatic Addison](https://automaticaddison.com/how-to-determine-what-torque-you-need-for-your-servo-motors/), [Free Robot Arm Payload Calculator, Firgelli Engineering](https://www.firgelliauto.com/blogs/engineering-calculators/robot-arm-payload-calculator-joint-torque)
- **Recalibration guidance (general, adjacent field but directly transferable practice):** a recommended verification step is to re-command previously calibrated endpoints after 24 hours of operation to confirm the output shaft still reaches the same positions and to check for calibration drift — attributed in search synthesis to general servo/precision-instrument calibration-verification practice; also, position drift is called out as commonly arising from internal potentiometer wear in heavily used (older/high-cycle-count) servos — [search synthesis on servo calibration drift verification]
- **Closed-loop compensation as the real-world fix (servo-specific idea):** for applications needing precision despite calibration drift, a commonly suggested approach is to add an external encoder that the controller reads, continuously adjusting the PWM command to correct for drift — effectively converting an open-loop RC servo into a closed-loop positioner, rather than relying on a single static calibration curve — [search synthesis on servo drift compensation via external encoder feedback]

### Inferences
- Because voltage sag's effect on torque/speed is well documented with concrete numbers (e.g., 3.3 kg·cm @ 4.8V vs. 4.1 kg·cm @ 6V for one servo model), and because insufficient torque under load directly causes position error when the servo cannot counter the load's torque demand, it follows that a single calibration curve taken unloaded and at nominal voltage may not hold at the extremes of a robot arm's operating envelope (heavy payload, sagging battery); load- and voltage-conditional recalibration (or closed-loop feedback) is the practical answer, though no source gives a servo-specific "X degrees of position error per Y grams of load" figure.
- Given that potentiometer TempCo is a documented general mechanism for position drift, and hobby servos use internal potentiometers for position feedback, temperature-driven calibration drift in RC servos is plausible and consistent with known electronics behavior, but no source measured this drift specifically for an RC servo in °C-to-µs or °C-to-degree terms.

### Gaps
- No source gave a concrete, servo-specific number for how many microseconds or degrees the center point/slope drifts per °C of temperature change, per volt of supply change, or per unit of applied load/torque. This is the weakest-evidenced sub-question in this whole research task; all quantitative material found was either (a) for a different domain (digital potentiometers' TempCo) or (b) about torque/speed spec changes rather than measured *position* drift.
- Could not retrieve full text of celeramotion's TN-1005 (Resolution/Accuracy/Repeatability) or the Elmo "servo drive voltage range" article to check for any more specific drift coefficients, since WebFetch was blocked for these domains in this session.

## How do people validate a fitted calibration curve afterward (held-out points, residuals/R², cross-validation, eyeballing)? What residual size is "good enough" for a hobby delta robot vs. precision robotics?

### Takeaway
Validation methodology is well documented in general calibration/metrology and machine-learning literature (residual-pattern inspection alongside R², lack-of-fit/Mandel's fitting tests, and held-out or cross-validated test points), and this transfers directly to a servo PWM/angle fit even though it is not servo-specific; for "good enough," the closest concrete anchor found is a widely cited R² ≥ 0.995 rule of thumb from analytical calibration practice (general, not servo-specific), plus a servo-community anecdotal range of roughly ±2–3° per joint as acceptable for hobby robot-arm applications — no source gave a distinct, explicit numeric threshold for "hobby delta robot" vs. "precision robotics" as a direct comparison.

### Cited Findings
- **R² is necessary but not sufficient (general calibration/metrology, not servo-specific):** "R² ≥ 0.995 is a widely cited rule of thumb for an acceptable analytical calibration curve, and some regulated methods hold to R² ≥ 0.999 or specify the correlation coefficient r rather than R². However, always look at the residual pattern alongside R², not instead of it." A high R² with a visibly curved residual plot indicates the linear range has been overstated, not that the fit passed — [CASRAI Calibration Curve guide](https://casrai.org/guides/analytical-calibration-curve-linear-range-back-calculation), [Linearity of Calibration Curves for Analytical Methods (IntechOpen)](https://www.intechopen.com/chapters/58596)
- **Residual pattern diagnostics (general, not servo-specific):** residuals for a properly-fit linear model should be normally/randomly distributed; systematic curvature suggests lack-of-fit from an unmodeled non-linear effect, and a segmented/fan-shaped residual pattern indicates heteroscedasticity (non-constant noise variance), in which case weighted regression is recommended over ordinary least squares — [IntechOpen Linearity review](https://www.intechopen.com/chapters/58596)
- **Formal lack-of-fit tests exist (general statistics, not servo-specific):** "the lack-of-fit test and Mandel's fitting test are suitable for validating a linear calibration model," used alongside t-values on the highest-order polynomial coefficient to decide whether a higher-order term is statistically justified — [IntechOpen Linearity review](https://www.intechopen.com/chapters/58596)
- **Held-out test points / cross-validation (general ML/metrology practice, directly transferable):** "a test set should still be held out for final evaluation... the paramount principle of cross-validation requires that no information from the hold-out test set be used when constructing the model." For low-cost sensor calibration specifically, results are commonly evaluated via RMSE on a held-out test set, and leave-one-out cross-validation is used when reference data is scarce — [scikit-learn cross-validation docs](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/cross_validation.html), [search synthesis on low-cost sensor calibration validation practice, incl. arXiv sensor-calibration studies]
- **Practical servo verification step (servo-specific but qualitative, not a numeric residual bound):** one recommended sanity check is to sweep the commanded pulse width from min to max and back, then command the nominal center pulse width (1500 µs) and confirm the output shaft returns to within 1–2° of the mechanical center — this is essentially a single repeatability/hysteresis check rather than a full residual analysis, but gives a concrete servo-specific numeric tolerance (1–2°) for "is my center calibration good enough" — [search synthesis on servo PWM sweep verification practice]
- **Acceptable positional error for hobby robot arms (servo-specific but sourced only via aggregated search synthesis, treat as lower-confidence):** with proper calibration, hobby-servo-based robot arms can achieve roughly 5–10 mm positional accuracy at ~500 mm reach, with sub-5 mm requiring dedicated calibration routines and error compensation; and per-joint calibration accuracy in the "±2–3°" range is described as generally acceptable for hobby robot-arm applications, though this is explicitly stated as task-dependent rather than a fixed standard — [search synthesis on DIY robotic arm accuracy, e.g. general robot-arm build guides]

### Inferences
- Combining the general-calibration R² ≥ 0.995 guideline with the servo-specific ±1–2° center-check tolerance and ±2–3°/joint "acceptable" range gives a reasonable, defensible target for a hobby delta-robot project: validate with a held-out set of points not used in the fit, require R² ≥ 0.99 (relaxing slightly from the analytical-chemistry 0.995 given servos are inherently noisier than lab instruments) *and* residuals on held-out points within roughly 1–3° (or the equivalent in µs, e.g., a few µs to ~10 µs for a servo spanning ~1000 µs over 180°), while precision robotics applications would need residuals at least an order of magnitude tighter and would rely on closed-loop encoder feedback rather than an open-loop PWM calibration curve alone. This synthesis is an inference bridging general and servo-specific sources — no single source states this comparison directly.
- Because general calibration-validation practice explicitly says R² alone is an unreliable pass/fail criterion (masking curvature or heteroscedasticity), any student project relying only on "eyeballing an R² value" without a residual plot risks over-trusting a fit that is actually poor near the range extremes — this is a general-methodology point, not servo-specific, but it applies directly and is worth flagging prominently in the final report.

### Gaps
- No source gave a directly stated, single numeric residual threshold explicitly distinguishing "hobby delta robot" from "precision robotics" — this comparison had to be synthesized/inferred from separate general-calibration and servo-specific numbers found in different sources, as flagged above.
- Could not retrieve the full mbedded.ninja "How To Calibrate Sensors" guide (blocked by WebFetch), which by its title looked like a strong general engineering source specifically covering sensor/actuator calibration validation methodology end-to-end — [mbedded.ninja Calibration guide](https://blog.mbedded.ninja/programming/signal-processing/calibration/) (title only, could not confirm content in this session)

## How many total calibration points is "enough" vs. overkill for a linear or low-order polynomial fit?

### Takeaway
General calibration-curve statistics (not servo-specific, but directly applicable to fitting any linear or low-order polynomial actuator model) treat about 5–6 evenly-spaced points as the practical floor for a defensible linear fit, with diminishing returns beyond roughly 8–12 points for a system already known to be close to linear; servo-specific practitioner writeups mostly use far fewer points (as few as 2–3 anchor points), relying on prior knowledge that hobby servo PWM-to-angle response is close to linear, and treat extra points mainly as a way to check for — not to correct for — curvature near the mechanical limits.

### Cited Findings
- General analytical-calibration guidance: "at least five non-zero concentrations is a practical working minimum for routine analytical work, with regulated bioanalytical method validation commonly calling for a blank plus at least six non-zero levels. More points, evenly spaced across the range, improve the reliability of the fitted slope and intercept and make curvature easier to spot." — [CASRAI Calibration Curve guide](https://casrai.org/guides/analytical-calibration-curve-linear-range-back-calculation)
- General guidance also stresses that the number of points needed depends on the required confidence in the slope/intercept and the acceptable risk of missing curvature, rather than a single fixed number — inspecting the calibration plot for outliers, influence points, and non-linear trends is described as the first, mandatory step regardless of point count — [IntechOpen Linearity of Calibration Curves review](https://www.intechopen.com/chapters/58596)
- On the minimal end, a two-point linear fit (using only two points from a known-linear region) was described elsewhere as producing acceptable average error for an unrelated fluid-level sensor application — illustrating that if a system's linear region is already well established, very few points can suffice for the *fit* itself; the risk is that a 2-point fit provides zero ability to check for curvature or identify a bad/outlier measurement — [search synthesis on minimal-point linear-fit practice, non-servo instrument context]
- Servo-specific practice (see also first key question above) shows real projects using as few as 2–3 points (min, max, center) up to roughly a dozen points every ~15° — [Servo Motor Calibration: Does It Matter? (dev.to)](https://dev.to/ihong95/servo-motor-calibration-does-it-matter-1685), [industrial PWM calibration guide](https://industrialmonitordirect.com/blogs/knowledgebase/rc-servo-pwm-pulse-width-calibration-safe-timing-limits)

### Inferences
- Synthesizing the general-calibration floor (5–6 points minimum, more to catch curvature) with servo-specific practice (2–3 points common when linearity is assumed, up to ~12 points when verifying it), a reasonable recommendation for a student project fitting a linear (or low-order polynomial, e.g. quadratic) model to a single servo is: use on the order of 7–13 calibration points evenly spaced across the usable angular range (enough to both fit a low-order polynomial with several residual degrees of freedom and visually/statistically check for curvature), then hold out 2–3 additional points (not used in the fit) purely for validation. Beyond roughly 15–20 points for a single-servo, single-model fit is likely overkill unless the servo is known/suspected to have significant non-linearity, in which case a higher point count would be needed to support a higher-order polynomial term with statistical confidence (per the lack-of-fit/Mandel's-test logic in the validation section above).
- The general "diminishing returns beyond ~8-12 points" framing is an inference from the metrology guidance that more points beyond an adequate floor mainly narrow confidence intervals rather than fundamentally change the fit — not a directly cited number for this specific case.

### Gaps
- No source gave a servo-specific numeric answer to "how many points is overkill" — this had to be inferred by combining the general analytical-calibration floor/ceiling framing with observed servo-community practice ranges. A dedicated servo-calibration methodology paper (e.g., the CMU-RI-TR-09-41 report, unretrievable in this session) likely has a more direct answer and should be checked directly.
