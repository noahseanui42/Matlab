# Servo Calibration Rig Designs (PWM → True Output Angle)

## What rig designs have hobbyists, students, or researchers actually built and documented?

### Takeaway
Documented builds cluster into four buildable families: (1) a 3D-printed protractor/notch jig with a pointed servo-arm read by eye or camera, (2) a magnetic/optical rotary encoder (e.g. AS5600) rigidly coupled to the servo output shaft with firmware that auto-sweeps and builds a lookup table, (3) fiducial-marker (AprilTag/ArUco) vision tracking of a tag mounted on the horn or on robot links, and (4) laser-pointer/wall projection, which shows up mainly as a pointing/tracking technique rather than a precision calibration method — no source documents it being used for PWM-to-angle calibration with reported accuracy.

### Cited Findings
- A common hobbyist rig aligns the servo so its horn/wiper rotates at the center of a printed protractor; the builder records the PWM value that lines the pointer up with each degree mark — this is the basis of the "servo Protractor (SG90)" 3D-printable dial and the "Protractor dial for micro servo" design shared on Thingiverse/Printables. — [Thingiverse: servo Protractor (SG90)](https://www.thingiverse.com/thing:4315300); [Printables: Protractor dial for micro servo](https://www.printables.com/model/132527-protractor-dial-for-micro-servo)
- Jeremy Lindsay's "Calibrating My Servos" write-up describes building a rig specifically to find "what PWM values turn servos to a specific angle," aligning the servo center with a simple protractor so the wiper position can be read off. — [Calibrating My Servos, Hackster.io](https://www.hackster.io/jeremy-lindsay/calibrating-my-servos-fa27ce)
- Ian Hong's "Exploring Servo Motor Calibration" (also posted to dev.to and Medium) uses a 3D-printed jig with notches every 15° and an arm with a pointed tip on the servo horn; the builder records the angle of each notch and the PWM value needed to align the pointer with it, repeating across the servo's full range to map PWM↔angle. — [Exploring Servo Motor Calibration, Hackster.io](https://www.hackster.io/ian-hong/exploring-servo-motor-calibration-414b95); mirrored at [Servo Motor Calibration: Does It Matter?, dev.to](https://dev.to/ihong95/servo-motor-calibration-does-it-matter-1685) and [Servo Calibration, Medium](https://medium.com/@ianqyhong/servo-calibration-4ea1d43c46a6)
- A University of Washington CSE466 student project (ServoCar) documents the same notch-jig approach: "The servo's position is measured using a 3D printed jig with notches every 15 degrees and an arm with a pointed tip," recording angle/PWM pairs at each notch across the whole range. — [UW CSE466 ServoCar calibration writeup](https://courses.cs.washington.edu/courses/cse466/01au/Projects/ServoCar/maxx/building/calibrate.pdf)
- On the Arduino Forum, a solved thread on "Servo calibration: angle versus microseconds" describes rotating the servo to its mechanical limit, placing the pointer/wiper against a standalone protractor (servos often exceed 180° of travel), and manually stepping a potentiometer or PWM value until the pointer lines up with each degree mark, logging the corresponding pulse width. — [Servo calibration: angle versus microseconds, Arduino Forum](https://forum.arduino.cc/t/solved-servo-calibration-angle-versus-microseconds/603175)
- A more automated, encoder-based rig ("Servo-Calibrator") replaces the protractor entirely with an AS5600 magnetic rotary encoder glued/mounted on the servo's output shaft as ground truth, driven by a browser-based Web Serial calibration wizard. — [vishwam-aggarwal/Servo-Calibrator, GitHub](https://github.com/vishwam-aggarwal/Servo-Calibrator)
- A vision-based rig for a hexapod project ("hexapod-tracker") mounts AprilTags (tag36h11 family) on robot joints/chassis and tracks them with one or more fixed overhead cameras (including USB webcams and iPhone Continuity Camera with LiDAR) to recover joint angles via pose estimation. — [lukas/hexapod-tracker, GitHub](https://github.com/lukas/hexapod-tracker)
- Laser-pointer + wall/frame projection appears in maker projects as a pan/tilt *pointing/tracking* mechanism (e.g., an Arduino laser turret with OpenCV motion detection), not as a servo PWM calibration method; the author explicitly notes the simple pixel→angle mapping used is only a linear approximation and is "inaccurate near the corners of the frame" because it ignores the true 3D geometry (laser-to-wall distance, wall dimensions). No write-up was found using laser/wall projection specifically to calibrate a servo's PWM-to-angle curve with reported accuracy. — [chenalan02/LaserPointerMotionDetection, GitHub](https://github.com/chenalan02/LaserPointerMotionDetection)
- A CMU Robotics Institute technical report, "Digital Servo Calibration and Modeling" (Tira-Thompson, CMU-RI-TR-09-41), exists as an academic treatment of servo calibration/modeling; its full methodology and reported accuracy could not be retrieved in this research pass (network access to ri.cmu.edu was blocked), so its specific rig design and numbers are not confirmed here. — [CMU-RI-TR-09-41 (title/existence only)](https://www.ri.cmu.edu/pub_files/2009/3/CMU-RI-TR-09-41.pdf)

### Inferences
- The overwhelming majority of documented, buildable, low-cost rigs are variations on "printed protractor + pointed horn," while the encoder-based and vision-based approaches are the ones people build specifically when they want to *automate* the measurement loop (avoid manually reading a dial hundreds of times across a full sweep).
- Laser+wall projection is a real, working technique for *aiming* a pan/tilt laser, and the underlying trigonometry (angle = atan(lateral offset / known laser-to-wall distance)) is sound and could in principle be repurposed to measure a servo's true rotation angle, but no one appears to have documented doing so for PWM calibration specifically — it seems to be an underused option in this space rather than a proven one.

### Gaps
- No description of a documented laser-pointer-and-wall rig purpose-built for *servo PWM calibration* (with reported angular accuracy) was found; existing laser/wall write-ups are for pointing/tracking, not calibration.
- The CMU technical report's actual rig, sensor, and accuracy figures could not be retrieved (site blocked); it is flagged as a lead for follow-up rather than a sourced finding.

## For a protractor + pointer + fixed camera setup: what accuracy is achieved, what error sources are reported, and what tips are given?

### Takeaway
Builders using printed-protractor rigs generally report treating "accuracy to the nearest degree" as good enough for hobby purposes, with parallax from manually eyeballing the pointer against the dial called out repeatedly as the dominant, well-known error source; the most-cited fix is to stop relying on a human eye/camera angle read entirely and substitute a sensor (encoder) for the dial, rather than to refine the photographic method.
- Note: none of the specific protractor+camera write-ups located in this pass state a quantitative angular error number for the photographic/pointer method itself (e.g., "±2°" or "±0.5°"); the "nearest degree" and parallax comments are qualitative. This is flagged in Gaps below.

### Cited Findings
- Search-engine synthesis of the protractor-rig write-ups states plainly: "accuracy to the nearest degree being good enough" was the working standard for the DIY protractor calibration approach. — [search synthesis of Hackster.io / related protractor write-ups](https://www.hackster.io/jeremy-lindsay/calibrating-my-servos-fa27ce)
- The same synthesis explicitly names the failure mode and the commonly suggested remedy: "Instead of manually reading values from the protractor, using a sensor to detect when the servo is in specific angular positions could eliminate parallax error." — [search synthesis re: Hackster.io protractor calibration](https://www.hackster.io/jeremy-lindsay/calibrating-my-servos-fa27ce)
- General measurement-science guidance surfaced alongside these results (not servo-specific) reinforces the same physical cause: to reduce parallax when reading an analog scale, view it exactly perpendicular/"straight-on," and precision protractor calibration standards themselves check readings against angle gauge blocks at multiple points (e.g., 15°, 30°, 45°, 90°) — the same logic that would apply to reading a servo-calibration dial. — [How to Reduce Parallax Error](https://answers.completeera.com/post/how-to-reduce-parallax-error-precision-tips-for-measurements); [Calibration of Angular Graduations/Protractor discussion, Elsmar Cove](https://elsmar.com/elsmarqualityforum/threads/calibration-of-angular-graduations-protractor.41663/)
- On low-cost hobby servos specifically, testers report the commanded-vs-actual relationship is not uniform across the range: angles from roughly 0°–50° are "stretched," 50°–90° are fairly accurate, and 90°–180° are "compressed" — meaning a protractor rig has to sample densely across the full sweep (not just endpoints/center) to catch this non-uniform error. — [search synthesis, Arduino/servo calibration discussion](https://forum.arduino.cc/t/solved-servo-calibration-angle-versus-microseconds/603175)
- No source in this pass reported using image-processing/automated photo analysis specifically on a protractor+pointer rig to read the angle (as opposed to a human reading the dial by eye or from a photo); the closest transferable technique found is a generic analog-gauge-reading OpenCV tool (covered under the marker/tools question below).

### Inferences
- Because the documented protractor rigs rely on a human eye (or a photo reviewed by a human) to judge when the pointer lines up with a degree mark, the realistic error budget is dominated by (a) parallax between the viewing/camera axis and the dial plane, (b) the angular subtense of the pointer tip and printed line width (a 1–2 mm wide printed line at a small radius easily represents 1–2° of arc), and (c) mechanical slop/flex in the printed horn extension acting as the pointer arm. None of the sources quantified these separately, but they are the mechanisms behind the qualitative "parallax" and "nearest degree" comments.
- A fixed, perpendicular camera (rather than a handheld phone photo) plus a printed crosshair/pointer tip sanded to a fine point would be expected to reduce these errors, consistent with the general parallax-reduction guidance found, but no source specifically validated this expectation on a servo rig with before/after numbers.

### Gaps
- No specific quantitative angular error/resolution number (e.g., "measured to ±X°") was found for any protractor+pointer+camera servo rig in the sources reachable during this research pass.
- No source discussed camera viewing-angle tolerance (how many degrees of camera misalignment causes how much parallax error) in the specific context of a servo-calibration protractor rig.
- No source described a specific automated image-processing pipeline applied to protractor+pointer photos for servo calibration (see the open-source-tools question below for the closest analog).

## How does a rotary encoder or potentiometer coupled to the servo shaft compare to camera+protractor in accuracy and setup effort?

### Takeaway
The one well-documented encoder-based rig (AS5600 magnetic encoder as ground truth) reports a large, specific accuracy win over a naive linear PWM↔angle model — cutting mean positioning error from 3.13° down to 0.19° with a 20-point calibration table — for a modest cost (~$10–15 encoder board) and moderate setup effort (wiring + a handful of Arduino libraries), which is a much stronger and better-quantified result than anything reported for the manual protractor approach.

### Cited Findings
- The Servo-Calibrator project mounts an AS5600 magnetic encoder on the servo's output shaft (magnet centered over the chip), read over I²C by an Arduino, as a continuous, ground-truth angle reference independent of the servo's own internal potentiometer. — [vishwam-aggarwal/Servo-Calibrator, GitHub](https://github.com/vishwam-aggarwal/Servo-Calibrator)
- Reported accuracy: "a 20-point table cut mean positioning error from 3.13° (naive linear) to 0.19° across five targets," and more broadly "a 20-point table cuts mean positioning error 2–6× vs. the linear formula" when tested across multiple physical servos. — [vishwam-aggarwal/Servo-Calibrator, GitHub](https://github.com/vishwam-aggarwal/Servo-Calibrator)
- Setup effort: install four Arduino libraries (an AS5600 driver plus three "Universal-Interface" repos), wire the servo signal to a microcontroller pin (e.g., A3) and the AS5600 to the I²C bus, provide external power, and serve a browser-based Web Serial calibration-wizard app locally (only needs Python or Node.js to host the HTML). — [vishwam-aggarwal/Servo-Calibrator, GitHub](https://github.com/vishwam-aggarwal/Servo-Calibrator)
- Algorithm: firmware runs a ~50 Hz state machine that coarse-then-fine "stall-scans" outward from center in both directions to find the servo's real mechanical limits, then sweeps the full range in both directions and averages the two sweeps into a 20-point lookup table, using small incremental pulse-width steps to avoid triggering non-linear responses from sudden large commanded jumps. — [vishwam-aggarwal/Servo-Calibrator, GitHub](https://github.com/vishwam-aggarwal/Servo-Calibrator)
- Cost: an AS5600 breakout board is inexpensive (roughly $10–15), and the only mechanical work needed is attaching a small magnet to the servo's output shaft. — [vishwam-aggarwal/Servo-Calibrator, GitHub](https://github.com/vishwam-aggarwal/Servo-Calibrator)
- General background on servo feedback: potentiometers sense angle via a rotation-proportional change in resistance and are the standard internal feedback element in hobby servos (e.g., SG90, MG996), while optical/magnetic encoders detect rotation via pulses/counts that must themselves be calibrated back to physical angle using a reference; broadly, potentiometer-based feedback (wiper wear, electrical noise, finite resistive-track linearity) is generally described as less precise/reliable than encoder-based feedback in servo-motor contexts. — [Servo Motor Position Feedback, Zbotic](https://zbotic.in/servo-motor-position-feedback-potentiometer-encoder-methods/); [Servo Motor Encoders, Quantum Devices](https://www.quantumdev.com/optical-encoder-applications/servo-motors/)

### Inferences
- The AS5600-based rig is a strictly better instrument for this task than the internal potentiometer already inside the hobby servo (which is not independently readable in most cheap servos anyway) and is dramatically better-characterized than the eyeball/protractor method: it is the only source in this research pass with a concrete before/after accuracy number (3.13° → 0.19°) for the exact task (PWM-to-angle calibration).
- Given the ~$10–15 cost and modest wiring/firmware effort, an encoder-coupled rig looks like the best accuracy-per-dollar/effort option among all documented approaches for someone who wants a repeatable, automatable calibration rather than a one-off manual reading.

### Inferences (cost/complexity tradeoff table, derived from cited findings)
| Approach | Approx. cost | Setup effort | Reported accuracy | Automatable? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Printed protractor + pointer, read by eye/camera | ~$0 (3D print + printed protractor) | Low (print jig, align, read by hand) | Qualitative "nearest degree"; parallax-limited, not quantified | No — needs a human to read each point |
| AS5600 magnetic encoder on shaft | ~$10–15 (encoder board + magnet) | Moderate (I²C wiring, a few libraries, firmware) | 3.13° (naive linear) → 0.19° (20-pt table), 2–6× better across servos tested | Yes — full sweep + lookup table built automatically |

### Gaps
- No source directly benchmarked a protractor+camera rig and an encoder rig side-by-side on the *same* servo under the *same* conditions; the comparison above is inferred from separate write-ups rather than a single controlled study.
- No specific numbers were found for potentiometer-only (non-encoder) external calibration rigs (i.e., bolting an external pot to the shaft rather than a magnetic encoder) — the sources found discuss the servo's own internal potentiometer as a feedback element, not an external calibration potentiometer rig.

## How do AprilTag/ArUco marker-based vision approaches work for measuring rotation angle, and what accuracy/repeatability is reported?

### Takeaway
Marker-based vision calibration works by detecting a fiducial (AprilTag or ArUco) rigidly attached to the servo horn/link with a calibrated camera, solving for the tag's 3D pose (typically via PnP) relative to a reference frame, and converting that pose to a rotation angle; documented real-world repeatability for this kind of low-cost, single/simplified-calibration setup is on the order of several degrees (not sub-degree), and is explicitly reported as sensitive to tag size, distance, and camera calibration quality.

### Cited Findings
- The hexapod-tracker project's pipeline: (1) a floor homography computed from floor-anchor tags gives physical x, y, yaw for markers on the ground plane; (2) a chassis tag establishes the robot body's orientation; (3) joint tags are observed and solved via a PnP (perspective-n-point) solver using documented mount-rotation offsets; (4) a kinematic consistency check rejects geometrically impossible pose branches (PnP/pose-from-planar-tag solutions are often ambiguous); (5) leg azimuth is computed by comparing the measured angle against a stored zero-pose reference. — [lukas/hexapod-tracker, GitHub](https://github.com/lukas/hexapod-tracker)
- Reported accuracy/repeatability for that system: a "provisional 5° 95% floor" for yaw-angle uncertainty, described as reflecting "observed stationary repeatability; simultaneous camera disagreement can raise it." The camera calibration used is explicitly called a simplified "constrained single-plane fit (centred principal point, square pixels, zero distortion)" and is labeled provisional/not metrology-grade without a full multi-pose calibration board procedure. — [lukas/hexapod-tracker, GitHub](https://github.com/lukas/hexapod-tracker)
- Consistent with that uncertainty budget, the project's own pose-verification tolerances are set at 12° (for a "two-lid" leg geometry) and 18° (for a "one-lid" leg) when zero-pose checking — i.e., the system is engineered around single-digit-to-double-digit-degree tolerances, not sub-degree precision. — [lukas/hexapod-tracker, GitHub](https://github.com/lukas/hexapod-tracker)
- A maintainer-unanswered AprilTag GitHub issue reports the opposite direction of concern: users measuring yaw/pitch/roll with tags constrained to ≤6 cm observe "fluctuations and noises" in the rotation estimate, i.e., small tag size directly degrades pose-angle stability; no resolution or concrete tag-size-vs-accuracy numbers were found in the visible discussion. — [AprilTag issue #58, AprilRobotics/apriltag, GitHub](https://github.com/AprilRobotics/apriltag/issues/58)
- For ArUco specifically, general calibration documentation and community results indicate detection accuracy depends strongly on marker size, viewing distance, and angle, with one cited result of "maximum error of 0.12 mm at 90° grazing angle" for position (this is a translational, not rotational, accuracy figure, so it should not be read as a rotation-angle error). ArUco is also noted as more versatile than a checkerboard for calibration because ArUco/ChArUco patterns tolerate partial occlusion and partial views. — [search synthesis of ArUco tracking/calibration results](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/389103265_Real-Time_Tracking_and_Distance_Measurement_of_Opencv_Aruco_Marker_Using_Webcam); [OpenCV: Calibration with ArUco and ChArUco](https://docs.opencv.org/4.13.0/da/d13/tutorial_aruco_calibration.html)
- Standard camera-calibration best practice repeatedly surfaced alongside these marker methods: accurate pose/angle recovery from AprilTag or ArUco requires the camera itself to be properly calibrated first (intrinsics + distortion), and calibration images should be taken across a range of positions and large angular differences to get a good calibration — sloppy or "constrained" camera calibration (as in the hexapod-tracker project) is called out as the reason that system's numbers stay in the several-degree range rather than sub-degree. — [MATLAB: Camera Calibration Using AprilTag Markers](https://www.mathworks.com/help/vision/ug/camera-calibration-using-apriltag-markers.html); [lukas/hexapod-tracker, GitHub](https://github.com/lukas/hexapod-tracker)
- One aggregator note mentions checkerboard/ArUco/AprilTag comparisons for camera calibration and general pose work, and references ArUco's ARUCO_BOX rig (three marker maps mounted on a precisely dimensioned non-planar box) as a technique specifically built for high-accuracy calibration rigs, though this is a camera-calibration rig, not a servo-angle-measurement rig per se. — [search synthesis re: ArUco camera calibration rigs](https://docs.opencv.org/4.13.0/da/d13/tutorial_aruco_calibration.html)

### Inferences
- Marker-based vision calibration trades away the manual-reading effort of a protractor rig for a fully automatable, continuous angle stream, but the one documented real-world implementation found here (hexapod-tracker) reports accuracy roughly an order of magnitude worse (single-digit degrees, with double-digit-degree tolerance windows) than the encoder-based rig's 0.19° result — the gap appears to trace mainly to camera-calibration rigor (a full multi-pose board calibration vs. a simplified single-plane fit) and tag size/distance, not to a fundamental limitation of the marker-tracking technique itself.
- A more careful camera calibration (proper multi-pose checkerboard/ChArUco intrinsics+distortion calibration, larger tags, closer/more-controlled camera-to-tag distance) would plausibly push marker-based angle accuracy well below the "5° floor" reported here, but no source in this pass demonstrated that improvement quantitatively for a servo-calibration use case specifically.

### Gaps
- No source gave a controlled, servo-specific AprilTag/ArUco calibration accuracy number under a properly calibrated (non-"provisional") camera setup — the hexapod-tracker numbers are the only concrete repeatability figures found, and the project's own documentation flags them as not metrology-grade.
- The AprilTag issue #58 discussion thread's actual maintainer responses/recommendations (tag family choice, size vs. distance tradeoffs) could not be retrieved — only the reporter's problem statement was visible in this pass.
- No source quantified ArUco/AprilTag rotation-angle error directly (as opposed to translational/position error, or generic "5° floor" repeatability); a marker-specific angular-error number (e.g., degrees of yaw error at a given tag size/distance) is a genuine gap.

## Are there open-source tools/scripts for turning a photo of a protractor+pointer into an angle measurement automatically?

### Takeaway
No open-source tool purpose-built for "photograph a protractor+pointer, get a servo angle" was found; the closest directly transferable prior art is a generic OpenCV/Python analog-gauge-reader that converts a photo of any needle-style dial into a numeric reading via line detection and arctangent geometry, plus several generic OpenCV angle-from-image techniques (contour-based, interactive click-based, and orientation-detection scripts) that could be adapted to this exact use case.

### Cited Findings
- `dgomes/analog-gauge-reader` (Python + OpenCV) reads a needle/pointer's angle from a photo of any analog dial gauge: it detects the needle as a line (filtering candidate lines via tunable difference-bound parameters), then computes the angle using trigonometry — "sine and cosine to plot the calibration image lines and arctangent to get the angle of the dial" — and maps that angle to a value using a calibration step (`python analog_gauge_reader.py --calibrate`, where the user records the min/max angle-to-value correspondence). — [dgomes/analog-gauge-reader, GitHub](https://github.com/dgomes/analog-gauge-reader)
- The same tool's documented caveats are directly relevant to adapting it to a protractor+pointer servo rig: "good lighting is key" with no shadows, and thin/small pointers/dials perform poorly compared with thicker ones — i.e., a thin printed servo-pointer needle may need to be widened or high-contrast for reliable automatic detection. — [dgomes/analog-gauge-reader, GitHub](https://github.com/dgomes/analog-gauge-reader)
- No accuracy/error numbers are published for the analog-gauge-reader tool itself, so its precision when applied to a servo pointer is unquantified. — [dgomes/analog-gauge-reader, GitHub](https://github.com/dgomes/analog-gauge-reader)
- Other generic, adaptable OpenCV angle-measurement scripts surfaced (not protractor-specific, but usable building blocks): an interactive 3-click angle measurement tool (click 3 points on any image, angle computed and displayed automatically), a contour-based angle-detection method (isolate a colored object in HSV space, extract its contour, compute angle via vector/inner-product math), and an object-orientation script based on OpenCV's own principal-component/orientation example code. — [Python-OpenCV Three Click & Measure Angle, HIGASARA-Blog](https://www.higashisalary.com/english/entry/python-calc-image-angle); [Easy calculation of Angle using OpenCV, Medium](https://medium.com/@elvenkim1/easy-calculation-of-angle-using-opencv-contours-and-coordinates-4f5e29851ea3); [How to Determine the Orientation of an Object Using OpenCV](https://automaticaddison.com/how-to-determine-the-orientation-of-an-object-using-opencv/)
- No MATLAB-specific open-source script for protractor/pointer photo angle-reading was found in this pass (the MATLAB results returned were about AprilTag-based *camera* calibration, not protractor-photo angle reading). — [MATLAB: Camera Calibration Using AprilTag Markers](https://www.mathworks.com/help/vision/ug/camera-calibration-using-apriltag-markers.html)

### Inferences
- Building a "photo of protractor+pointer → angle" tool would not require new computer-vision research: it is a straightforward re-application of the analog-gauge-reader's needle-detection-plus-arctangent approach (or the simpler 3-click/contour scripts), the main adaptation work being tuning line/contour detection to the specific printed pointer geometry and lighting, and replacing its value-lookup calibration step with a direct degrees-out-of-360 readout.
- The complete absence of an existing purpose-built tool suggests this is a small, low-effort gap in the hobbyist ecosystem rather than a hard problem — most builders documented so far chose to read the dial by eye (protractor rigs) or bypass photography entirely with an encoder (AS5600 rigs) rather than automate the photo-reading step.

### Gaps
- No servo-calibration-specific "protractor photo → angle" open-source script exists in any source found in this research pass; the analog-gauge-reader repo is the closest available prior art and would require adaptation, not direct reuse.
- No accuracy/error numbers exist for applying image-processing angle-reading (gauge-reader or otherwise) to a servo pointer specifically — this remains untested/undocumented territory based on available sources.
