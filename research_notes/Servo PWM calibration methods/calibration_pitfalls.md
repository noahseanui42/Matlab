# Servo PWM Calibration Pitfalls (Direct Microcontroller Drive, No PCA9685)

## How significant is servo horn/gear backlash in practice, and how does it show up as a calibration error?

### Takeaway
Backlash in cheap hobby servos is on the order of roughly 0.5–2+ degrees at the output, driven mainly by plastic-gear tolerance and worsened by spline/horn mounting play; it manifests in calibration as direction-dependent position error (hysteresis) — the same commanded pulse width yields a different actual angle depending on whether the servo last moved clockwise or counter-clockwise into that command — and it is easy to mistake for other error sources (linkage flex, control-loop lag) unless tested specifically for directionality.

### Cited Findings
- Comparing a plastic-gear SG90 to a metal-gear MG90S: "When commanded to return to 90°, the MG90S will typically return within 0.5° while the SG90 may vary by 1–2° due to gear backlash," attributed to plastic vs. metal gear machining tolerance — [Zbotic MG90S vs SG90](https://zbotic.in/mg90s-vs-sg90-torque-accuracy-best-uses-compared/)
- Genuine (non-counterfeit) TowerPro MG996R units are reported as "much better made (less slop in the gearing) than a pair of fakes," indicating counterfeit/clone servos can have materially worse backlash than the same nominal part number from a reputable source — [component/community discussion via search synthesis, MG996R](https://components101.com/motors/mg996r-servo-motor-datasheet)
- Backlash is formally defined as "a clearance or lost motion in a mechanism caused by gaps between parts... an angle or distance through which any part of a mechanical system may move in one direction without applying force or motion to the next part" — this is the textbook description of the direction-dependent error calibration routines will see — [Valin: Understanding and Mitigating Backlash](https://www.valin.com/resources/blog/understanding-and-mitigating-backlash-mechanical-systems)
- A two-way (forward/backward) scanning calibration method estimates backlash directly via `2·φ_backlash = φ_peak_forward − φ_peak_backward`, i.e., by commanding the same nominal position from both directions of approach and taking half the difference — [ScienceDirect: backlash measurement in low-cost UART servo motors](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2468067226000271)
- "Having increased resolution in the backlash zone is of no value as the gear output does not move" — within the backlash dead zone, finer pulse-width steps do not produce finer angular steps, so PWM resolution improvements cannot compensate for backlash — [RoboticsTomorrow: Resolution, Accuracy & Repeatability](https://www.roboticstomorrow.com/article/2023/10/what-are-resolution-accuracy-repeatability/21329)
- Separately from gearbox backlash, spline/horn mounting play is its own error source: users report "a little play on the [servo horn] spline" and that horns are "not all indexed the same" — one aileron horn was reportedly not at true 90° when centered, causing asymmetric throw purely from horn-to-spline seating, not from the gear train itself — [RCTech forum: Servo Horn Has A Little Play On Spline](https://www.rctech.net/forum/radio-electronics/588982-servo-horn-has-little-play-spline.html); [Hobby Squawk: Servo horn spline indexing](https://www.hobbysquawk.com/forum/electronics-and-rc-accessories/tools-and-accessories/120498-servo-horn-spline-indexing)
- Diagnostically, backlash (nonlinear, direction-dependent, appears only at direction reversals) is frequently confused with linear mechanical compliance (shaft twist, coupling flex, belt stretch) or with control-loop-induced oscillation, because "control loop problems cause behavior that mimics mechanical backlash perfectly" and "a failing encoder can produce the same positioning errors as a worn gearbox" — [GES Repair: Backlash in Servo Systems Isn't Always Mechanical](https://gesrepair.com/backlash-in-servo-systems-isnt-always-mechanical)

### Inferences
- For a direct-PWM calibration routine (no external position sensor), backlash is invisible unless the routine deliberately approaches each calibration point from both directions; a naive monotonic sweep (0→180°) will silently bake in one-directional backlash into the fitted pulse-width-to-angle curve, which will then read wrong whenever the servo is commanded from the other side.
- Because horn/spline seating error is mechanically independent of the servo's internal gear train, re-seating a horn (even on the same servo) can shift the effective zero point by roughly a spline-tooth's worth of angle, meaning calibration constants are tied to a specific horn-mounting instance, not just the servo unit.
- Backlash numbers found (0.5–2°) suggest that for many practical hobby-servo pointing tasks, backlash alone can exceed the angular resolution achievable from microsecond pulse-width control (see next question), meaning backlash — not PWM quantization — is likely the dominant calibration error budget item for typical analog/cheap-digital servos.

### Gaps
- No manufacturer datasheet in the results gave an explicit backlash spec in degrees for a standard hobby servo (e.g., SG90, MG996R, or Hitec/Futaba parts); the 0.5–2° figures come from comparative product-page/community commentary, not a datasheet tolerance spec.
- No quantified numbers found specifically isolating spline/horn play in degrees (only qualitative "a little play" reports).

## Is the "zero"/center/home position non-repeatable across sessions, and why?

### Takeaway
Yes — center/home position repeatability is limited primarily by unit-to-unit and even session-to-session variation in the servo's internal potentiometer (resistance tolerance, wiper-to-shaft mounting tolerance, comparator reference-voltage tolerance) and by wear/contamination of the pot's resistive track, not by anything the microcontroller does; the standard workaround is per-servo (and, for high-precision needs, per-session) software calibration rather than trusting a universal "1500 µs = center" assumption.

### Cited Findings
- "Two servos of the same model number can produce measurably different output shaft positions for identical pulse widths because of potentiometer resistance tolerance (+/- 10-20% common in low-cost servos), potentiometer wiper mounting tolerance relative to the output shaft, and manufacturing variance in the comparator reference voltage" — [search synthesis citing Pololu / RC servo interface discussions](https://www.pololu.com/blog/17/servo-control-interface-in-detail)
- "The actual mechanical angle produced depends on the internal potentiometer's electrical range, the gear reduction ratio, and the linkage geometry between the motor and output shaft. Manufacturers trim these values to give a marketing angle (often 120°, 180°, or 270°) while leaving additional internal potentiometer travel as a safety margin" — meaning the nominal "180° servo" spec is not a tight guarantee of actual travel-per-microsecond — [industry knowledgebase article via search](https://industrialmonitordirect.com/blogs/knowledgebase/rc-servo-pwm-pulse-width-calibration-safe-timing-limits)
- "For systems requiring absolute positional repeatability, device-to-device variation must be absorbed by a calibration step or by a closed-loop position control system" — the recommended fix is calibration/closed-loop feedback, not relying on nominal pulse-width-to-angle mappings — [same source above]
- Tiny potentiometers in micro servos have wipers that travel over miniature resistive strips, and "any wear, contamination, or oxidation on this strip creates a disproportionate dead zone" — implying the center position can drift over the servo's lifetime/usage, not just differ unit-to-unit at manufacture — [Industrial Monitor Direct: servo position controller / potentiometer feedback discussion](https://industrialmonitordirect.com/blogs/knowledgebase/building-dc-servo-motor-position-controller-with-pot-feedback)
- A common reported real-world symptom: "telling a servo to go to 0 degrees, it might move to 130 degrees instead" and standard `Servo.write(angle)` mapping is inconsistent across the range — "stretched" from 0–50°, roughly accurate 50–90°, "compressed" 90–180° — pointing to a non-linear/offset relationship between commanded angle and actual angle that must be characterized per unit — [Arduino Forum threads, synthesized](https://forum.arduino.cc/t/solved-servo-calibration-angle-versus-microseconds/603175)

### Inferences
- Because the root cause (potentiometer tolerance/wear) is internal and analog, it is not something firmware can fix generically — each servo effectively needs its own calibration table (min/center/max pulse width, and ideally a multi-point curve), and that table should be treated as tied to the individual physical unit, not the model number.
- Pot wear/contamination implies calibration is not necessarily stable for the life of the servo — a calibration done once at the start of a project could drift after significant use, which is a maintenance/recalibration consideration for long-running systems.

### Gaps
- No source gave a quantified session-to-session drift figure in microseconds or degrees for center position on a single unmodified servo (i.e., how much does the "same" servo's center wander between power cycles, absent wear) — this remains characterized only qualitatively ("can vary") rather than numerically.

## Does Arduino-generated PWM (Servo library or bit-banging) fail to match servo-expected timing — jitter, refresh rate, resolution — and what symptoms result?

### Takeaway
The Arduino Servo library's default 50 Hz / 20 ms refresh rate is standard, but its software-timer-interrupt-based pulse generation is subject to jitter from interrupt contention (especially with multiple servos or other libraries competing for the same hardware timer), and its microsecond resolution is not guaranteed to be 1 µs on all boards; symptoms reported include visible/audible jitter, dither, and — on at least one modern board — coarser step sizes than expected.

### Cited Findings
- Default Arduino Servo library refresh interval is 20,000 µs (20 ms), i.e., standard 50 Hz servo control frequency — [search synthesis of Arduino Servo library documentation/forum discussion](https://forum.arduino.cc/t/servo-library-20ms-refresh-instead-of-14ms-why/105615)
- "The original servo library uses prescaler 8 for timer 1, which gives a resolution of 1/2 microsecond steps. However, using prescaler 1 instead provides a resolution of 1/16 microsecond, which can reduce jitter significantly" — implying the stock library's timer configuration is not the finest resolution achievable on the same hardware — [Lamja.com: Arduino UNO Controlling 20 Servos With 15-bit Precision And Low Jitter](http://www.lamja.com/?p=504)
- "When multiple PWMs are enabled simultaneously, timer interrupts can interfere with each other, causing the duty error range to reach up to 77 microseconds, which is sufficient for low-resolution servos to respond and generate visible jitter" — a concrete jitter magnitude (up to ~77 µs) reported for a multi-PWM contention scenario — [QEC: Comparison of Arduino and 86Duino Servo Library PWM Jitter Phenomena](https://www.qec.tw/86duino-library/servo/comparison-of-arduino-and-86duino-servo-library-pwm-jitter-phenomena/)
- "The built-in Servo library uses software emulation to implement multiple PWM signals, and the PWM generated by software simulation is different from hardware PWM, most commonly resulting in PWM jitter" and "a software timer approach will always be challenging, especially if other functionality is added, making it a fundamental limitation rather than a bug" — framing jitter as an inherent property of interrupt-driven software PWM generation, not a fixable defect — [same QEC source above]
- Arduino Servo library source (Servo.cpp, AVR core, PaulStoffregen/arduino Servo library): timer ticks are computed via `usToTicks(_us) = (clockCyclesPerMicrosecond() * _us) / 8` (8x prescaler), with a `TRIM_DURATION = 2` tick correction "to trim adjust for digitalWrite delays" compensating for ISR/pin-toggle latency; `MIN_PULSE_WIDTH` = 544 µs and `MAX_PULSE_WIDTH` = 2400 µs are the library's built-in default clamp range (i.e., calling `Servo.write()`/`writeMicroseconds()` outside this range is clamped unless the sketch explicitly attaches with custom min/max); servos are grouped and time-multiplexed onto shared 16-bit hardware timers, with pulses issued serially (never simultaneously) from a common T=0 reference so that motion across channels stays synchronized — [github.com/PaulStoffregen/Servo, Servo.cpp](https://github.com/PaulStoffregen/Servo/blob/master/Servo.cpp)
- On the Arduino Nano Every, `writeMicroseconds()` resolution is reported as only 4 µs increments, versus 1 µs increments on the classic Uno — i.e., pulse-width (and thus angular) resolution can be markedly worse on newer/different MCU cores despite using the "same" Servo library API — [Arduino Forum: Limited writeMicroseconds() resolution when using Servo Library - Nano Every](https://forum.arduino.cc/t/limited-writemicroseconds-resolution-when-using-servo-library/657592)
- Analog servos (50 Hz native design) "may become erratic or jitter when fed signals above 100Hz because their control circuit is not designed for such high-frequency updates" — a mismatch-direction symptom: sending too-fast/non-standard refresh rates (e.g., from bit-banged or misconfigured PWM) to an analog servo can itself cause jitter/erratic behavior, independent of the microcontroller's own timing jitter — [Zbotic: Digital Servo vs Analog Servo](https://zbotic.in/digital-servo-vs-analog-servo-speed-accuracy-power-differences/)

### Inferences
- For calibration purposes, jitter of tens of microseconds (e.g., ~77 µs reported under multi-servo contention) is on the same order as, or larger than, the pulse-width change needed for 1° of motion on many servos (see resolution section below) — meaning under contention, jitter alone can be a bigger source of positional noise during calibration reads than the intended pulse-width step size.
- Because resolution and jitter both trace back to how the specific board's timer/core implements the library (8-bit vs 16-bit timer, prescaler choice, core clock), calibration code that assumes "1 µs = 1 unit of control" is implicitly assuming Uno/Mega-class AVR timer behavior; on other boards (Nano Every and other non-classic-AVR cores) that assumption can be wrong by a factor of several µs, which would need to be verified per target board rather than assumed.
- Bit-banging PWM directly (bypassing the Servo library) inherits the same fundamental problem (software timing under interrupt/loop jitter) unless a dedicated hardware PWM/timer channel with no competing ISRs is used — the library's own architecture (shared 16-bit timer, serialized pulses, ISR-based edge timing) is essentially a more careful version of what a naive bit-bang would do less carefully.

### Gaps
- No source gave a directly measured jitter figure for a single-servo, single-library-instance case (i.e., baseline jitter with only one servo attached and no contention) — the ~77 µs figure is specifically for a multi-PWM-simultaneous scenario, and it's unclear from the results what the jitter floor is for the simplest one-servo case.
- The exact value of `SERVOS_PER_TIMER` (how many servos share one hardware timer before another is engaged) and `DEFAULT_PULSE_WIDTH` were referenced in the source comments but their literal numeric values were not visible in the fetched excerpt.

## What angular resolution limit does microsecond-level pulse-width rounding impose, and is it practically limiting?

### Takeaway
For a typical hobby servo with roughly 1000 µs of pulse-width span mapped to 180° of travel (~5.56 µs/degree), 1 µs of pulse-width resolution corresponds to about 0.18° of angular resolution — and on boards where the library's real resolution is coarser (e.g., 4 µs on Nano Every, or ~4 µs floor cited for 16 MHz timers at 50 Hz), the resulting resolution is roughly 0.7°; in both cases this is generally smaller than backlash and potentiometer-tolerance error, so microsecond quantization is usually not the limiting factor for direct-PWM calibration accuracy — mechanical/electrical tolerances dominate instead.

### Cited Findings
- "A servo with a 180° range and 1000–2000 µs travel has approximately 5.56 µs per degree" (1000 µs ÷ 180°) — [search synthesis of servo PWM/timer resolution discussion](https://joe.blog.freemansoft.com/2023/01/servo-pulse-width-modulation-pwm-timer.html)
- "At 50 Hz, the minimum step an Arduino's 16 MHz timer can reliably produce is around 4 µs, so the theoretical angular resolution is roughly 0.7°" — [same source above]
- For higher-resolution timer configurations (example given: TCC prescaler setup on an ATSAMD21-class MCU), "533.33 counts per degree, or 0.001875° (6.75 arc-seconds) per count" is achievable — showing that with a better-suited timer/prescaler the pulse-width quantization limit can be pushed far below any mechanically meaningful resolution — [Industrial Monitor Direct: ATSAMD21 Servo PWM Resolution Math](https://industrialmonitordirect.com/blogs/knowledgebase/atsamd21-servo-pwm-resolution-and-pid-control-for-rocket-flight)
- On Nano Every, the Servo library's real-world `writeMicroseconds()` resolution is 4 µs (vs. 1 µs on Uno) — at ~5.56 µs/degree this is ≈0.72°/step on that board specifically — [Arduino Forum: Limited writeMicroseconds() resolution](https://forum.arduino.cc/t/limited-writemicroseconds-resolution-when-using-servo-library/657592)
- Digital servos "can have their deadband programmed down to 1 microsecond or lower, providing a 5x improvement in resolution" relative to analog servos — implying the servo's own internal deadband/resolution can itself be the binding constraint below which finer microsecond commands have no effect, separate from the MCU's ability to produce them — [Zbotic: Digital Servo vs Analog Servo](https://zbotic.in/digital-servo-vs-analog-servo-speed-accuracy-power-differences/)

### Inferences
- Comparing figures: 1 µs pulse resolution (Uno) ≈ 0.18°/step; 4 µs (Nano Every, or generic 16 MHz timer floor) ≈ 0.7°/step; measured backlash was 0.5–2° (SG90/MG90S comparison above). This means on a classic AVR board (1 µs resolution), pulse-width quantization (~0.18°) is smaller than backlash (0.5–2°) — so backlash, not µs rounding, is the practical accuracy ceiling. On a board with only 4 µs resolution, quantization (~0.7°) becomes comparable to or larger than backlash on the tighter-backlash servo (MG90S ~0.5°), meaning board choice can shift which error source dominates.
- Because deadband (the servo's own internal "don't bother moving for a change smaller than X µs" threshold) can be on the order of 1 µs or more even for "high resolution" digital servos, and can be much larger (unspecified, but implied non-trivial) for analog servos, the effective end-to-end resolution of a calibration step is bounded by max(MCU pulse-width step size, servo internal deadband, backlash) — not by the MCU alone.

### Gaps
- No source gave a typical analog-servo deadband number in µs for comparison against the "digital servos down to 1 µs" figure — only a qualitative "analog servos have lower resolution" claim was found.
- No source directly stated whether the commonly-quoted "0.18°/µs" style resolution number is ever the dominant error term in a real reported calibration effort (this is an inference in this report, not something a cited source stated outright).

## Do analog and digital servos differ meaningfully in linearity, backlash, resolution, or update rate for calibration purposes?

### Takeaway
Yes: digital servos use a much higher internal update/pulse rate to the motor (reported ~300 Hz and up, vs. analog's native 50 Hz), which gives finer positional resolution, programmable deadband down to ~1 µs, faster response, and generally less backlash/better holding torque than analog servos of similar size — but digital servos are also reported as more expensive and more power-hungry, and analog servos can become erratic if fed refresh rates much above 100 Hz, which is a relevant caution if a calibration routine sends non-standard refresh rates.

### Cited Findings
- "Analog servos receive signals and output power at a frequency of 50Hz... In contrast, digital servos use a microprocessor to process receiver signals and send very high frequency voltage pulses to the motor at 300 and up pulses per second," with some sources citing rates "around 500Hz" — [Zbotic: Digital Servo vs Analog Servo](https://zbotic.in/digital-servo-vs-analog-servo-speed-accuracy-power-differences/)
- "The higher update rate of a digital servo improves steadiness and makes the servo feel faster when moving from one position to another. However, analog servos may become erratic or jitter when fed signals above 100Hz because their control circuit is not designed for such high-frequency updates" — [same source]
- "Digital servos can have their deadband programmed down to 1 microsecond or lower, providing a 5x improvement in resolution [over analog]" and "Digital servos have much improved deadband, better resolution, faster response, quicker and smoother acceleration/deceleration, and immensely better holding [torque] compared to analog servos" — [same source]
- Metal-gear/digital MG90S showed ~0.5° return-to-center variation vs. plastic-gear/analog-drive SG90's 1–2° (see backlash section) — a concrete example consistent with "digital/better-built servos have less backlash," though this comparison conflates the digital-vs-analog control distinction with the plastic-vs-metal-gear distinction, so it is not a clean isolated comparison of control type alone — [Zbotic MG90S vs SG90](https://zbotic.in/mg90s-vs-sg90-torque-accuracy-best-uses-compared/)

### Inferences
- Because digital servos' higher internal update rate mainly affects response speed/smoothness and deadband (not the coarse pulse-width-to-angle mapping the calibration curve captures), the calibration procedure itself (finding min/center/max pulse widths and the mapping in between) does not need to differ structurally between analog and digital servos — but digital servos will likely show tighter repeatability (smaller residual scatter) around each calibration point, and analog servos will tolerate a narrower range of refresh rates from a bit-banged or non-Servo-library PWM source.
- The "digital = less backlash" pattern found is confounded with "digital servos tend to also have metal gears" in the sources reviewed; a report writer should flag that the causal driver of lower backlash in the cited comparison is more likely gear material/quality than the analog/digital control distinction per se.

### Gaps
- No source separated "digital vs analog control electronics" from "plastic vs metal gears" cleanly enough to attribute the backlash difference purely to control type; this is a confound to note rather than a settled fact.
- No datasheet-level linearity spec (e.g., "±X% linearity error across travel") was found for either analog or digital servo classes.

## Other commonly reported mistakes in building a servo calibration routine

### Takeaway
Beyond backlash/repeatability/PWM-resolution issues, the most commonly reported practical mistakes are: powering servos from the same rail as the microcontroller and triggering brownout resets from motor current spikes; not waiting for the servo to physically settle before trusting/reading its position; assuming the nominal "0–180°"/"1000–2000 µs" spec applies uniformly across the whole range when real units are non-linear or clamp early at the extremes; and misdiagnosing linkage/mounting compliance (twisting shafts, flexing couplings, worn splines) as servo backlash or servo error.

### Cited Findings
- "The signature failure when an SG90 shares the Arduino 5V rail is a brownout reset: the ATmega328P loses regulation on Vcc, the BOD (Brown-Out Detector) trips, and the firmware restarts. When the PWM command changes and the motor accelerates, the DC motor inside draws a current spike of 2-3× the steady-state value for 10-50 ms" — [Industrial Monitor Direct: Powering SG90 Servo from Arduino Direct 5V Rail](https://industrialmonitordirect.com/blogs/knowledgebase/powering-sg90-servo-from-arduino-direct-5v-rail-design-guide)
- Recommended fix: "Place a 470 µF or 1000 µF electrolytic capacitor across the servo power supply rails, as close to the servo as possible" to absorb current spikes and prevent voltage droops that reset the Arduino; also noted that with USB-only input, the practical current ceiling to the servo is "~450 mA," and that shared ground between servo supply and Arduino ground is mandatory — [same source above]
- A brownout/reset during a calibration sweep would present as the calibration routine mysteriously restarting, losing its calibration-in-progress state, or producing garbage/inconsistent readings exactly when the servo is commanded to move a large distance or reverse direction — the current-spike mechanism described above is direction-of-motion/acceleration-dependent, which is exactly when a calibration sweep issues new setpoints — [same source, inference from mechanism described]
- Typical calibration/settle-time practice: "a 2-second delay to ensure the servo has time to reach its position and settle" is used before trusting a position in example calibration code, and calibration code commonly uses a similar ~2-second sleep after moving to each test angle before recording/reading results — [search synthesis of MicroPython/Adafruit-style servo calibration tutorials](https://docs.micropython.org/en/latest/pyboard/tutorial/servo.html)
- "For some angles, the returned angle is not exactly the same as the angle you set, due to rounding errors in setting the pulse width" — a documented library-level caveat that the angle "read back" (which in most hobby-servo-without-feedback setups is really just the last commanded angle, not a true sensor reading) can already disagree with what was requested purely from software rounding, before any mechanical error is added — [MicroPython servo tutorial documentation](https://docs.micropython.org/en/latest/pyboard/tutorial/servo.html)
- Manufacturing variance means calibration must be treated per-unit: "Servos are notoriously inaccurate, and you'll likely still need some sort of calibration in software," with reports of commanding 0° and getting ~130° instead, and of the angle-to-pulse-width relationship being "stretched" at one end of the range and "compressed" at the other rather than uniformly linear — a common practical response is building a lookup table of measured (pulse-width, actual-angle) pairs across several points and interpolating/curve-fitting, rather than trusting a single linear min/max mapping — [Arduino Forum threads, synthesized](https://forum.arduino.cc/t/solved-servo-calibration-angle-versus-microseconds/603175)
- Diagnostic pitfall: linear mechanical compliance (shaft twist, coupling flex, belt/linkage stretch) can produce symptoms — position error that appears/disappears with direction and load — that are easily confused with true (nonlinear) gear backlash; distinguishing the two requires recognizing that true backlash is nonlinear (a dead zone) while compliance-based error scales roughly linearly with applied torque/load — [GES Repair: Backlash in Servo Systems Isn't Always Mechanical](https://gesrepair.com/backlash-in-servo-systems-isnt-always-mechanical)

### Inferences
- Because the current-spike/brownout mechanism is specifically tied to motion (acceleration draws 2-3x steady-state current for 10-50 ms), a calibration routine that steps through many setpoints in quick succession is a worst-case stress test for a shared, uncapacitated power rail — this is a strong argument for either a separate servo power supply or a bulk capacitor directly at the servo, specifically during calibration/sweep testing, even if the same servo seems to behave fine during light, infrequent use.
- Since "read back angle" from a plain hobby servo without position feedback is just the last commanded value (not a real sensor reading), any calibration routine that treats the commanded angle as ground truth for verification is not actually testing the servo's true output angle at all — genuine calibration validation requires an external reference (protractor, camera, potentiometer/encoder tap, or an analog-feedback servo variant) rather than trusting the library's return value.
- Settle-time guidance in examples (~2 seconds) is generously conservative for typical small hobby servos (which usually complete a fast, unloaded 60° move in a few hundred milliseconds per manufacturer speed specs); the practical lesson is not a specific universal number but the general mistake of reading/logging a position too soon after a large commanded step, before mechanical settling and any overshoot/oscillation has died out.

### Gaps
- No source gave a measured, servo-specific settle/rise time number (e.g., "X ms to settle within Y° of commanded position for a 60° step") to replace the generic "wait ~2 seconds" heuristic — actual settle time depends heavily on servo speed rating, load, and step size, and no source quantified this beyond example code choosing a round conservative delay.
- No source directly documented a case study of "linkage flex mistaken for servo backlash" in a hobby/Arduino-servo-specific calibration writeup (the flex-vs-backlash confusion material found was from general industrial servo-motor troubleshooting, not hobby RC-servo calibration specifically) — this is a plausible-but-not-hobby-specific-sourced gotcha, and it is presented as an adjacent/analogous industrial finding rather than one purpose-confirmed for Arduino/hobby servo calibration.
