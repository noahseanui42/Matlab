# Servo PWM-to-Angle Response: Linearity, Deadband, Backlash/Hysteresis, and Modeling Practice

## Do standard analog and digital RC servos show measurable deviation from a linear pulse-width-vs-angle relationship, and how large is it (degrees/µs)?

### Takeaway
RC servo pulse-width-to-angle response is described by nearly every practitioner source as "strongly" or "pretty" linear in the coarse sense, but empirical calibration work consistently finds enough real deviation that a single global linear fit leaves multiple degrees of residual error — and unit-to-unit variation (even within the same model) is large enough that per-unit calibration is treated as mandatory, not optional.

### Cited Findings
- The standard hobby servo PWM envelope is nominally 1000 µs → 0°, 1500 µs → 90° (center), 2000 µs → 180°, but "the relationship between pulse width and angle varies widely by servo model, and there is even variation between units of the same model, creating the need for calibration to maximize accuracy" — [Exploring Servo Motor Calibration, Hackster.io](https://www.hackster.io/ian-hong/exploring-servo-motor-calibration-414b95) (via search summary; page itself was unreachable due to network egress restrictions in this session)
- "The relationship often looks pretty linear, which means you can easily calculate the pulse width required for any servo angle using the line of best fit" but "if the relationship was perfectly linear, the difference in PWM values at any fixed angle increment would be the same throughout the range, though variations may be observed" (i.e., the author explicitly measured non-constant slope segment-to-segment) — [Exploring Servo Motor Calibration, Hackster.io](https://www.hackster.io/ian-hong/exploring-servo-motor-calibration-414b95)
- Even two servos of the identical model can show ±0.1 ms (±100 µs) variation in the pulse widths corresponding to the same commanded angles, which is why each unit should be swept individually (0.5–2.5 ms) and its 0°/180° pulse widths recorded rather than assumed — [DEV Community / Hackster.io, servo calibration write-ups](https://dev.to/ihong95/servo-motor-calibration-does-it-matter-1685) (via search summary)
- A from-scratch, ground-truth-instrumented calibrator (AS5600 magnetic encoder on the output shaft, used as the true-angle reference instead of trusting the commanded angle) found that a **single linear formula produced a mean positioning error of 3.13° across five test targets**, while replacing the linear formula with a 20-point lookup table cut that to **0.19° mean error** — roughly a 16x reduction for that unit, and "a 20-point table cuts mean positioning error 2–6× vs. the linear formula" when tested across two different servos — [vishwam-aggarwal/Servo-Calibrator, GitHub](https://github.com/vishwam-aggarwal/Servo-Calibrator)
- Some servos are explicitly called out as being unusually linear/accurate — e.g. "the DS3235 are pretty accurate and linear, so you may not need a full calibration if you know the pulse width difference between two known positions" — implying this is the exception rather than the rule for hobby-grade units — [search result summary, servo calibration discussion](https://www.hackster.io/jeremy-lindsay/calibrating-my-servos-fa27ce)
- One academic characterization paper, "SG90 Servo Characterization," measured the SG90's transient and steady-state response using an IMU on the output shaft and reports finding "instabilities" during testing and derives a transfer function — indicating measurable non-ideal, non-purely-linear dynamic behavior for this ubiquitous micro servo — [SG90 Servo Characterization (ResearchGate/Academia.edu)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/353754375_SG90_Servo_Characterization) (full text not retrievable in this session — see Gaps)

### Inferences
- The 3.13° → 0.19° result (16x, and 2–6x across a small sample) is the most concrete, directly-measured magnitude found for how much a single global linear fit under-performs a denser calibration on real hobby servo hardware; it suggests a plain linear fit's error budget for a single unit is on the order of a few degrees (roughly 2–4°) across the usable range, concentrated in curvature away from the calibration endpoints rather than being a uniform offset.
- Because unit-to-unit variation (±100 µs quoted) is comparable in scale to the nonlinearity itself, any calibration scheme — linear or higher-order — must be fit per physical unit; a factory nominal linear formula (1000/1500/2000 µs) is not a substitute for measuring each servo.

### Gaps
- No manufacturer datasheet (Hitec, Futaba, TowerPro, Dynamixel) with an explicit numeric "linearity" or "%FSO deviation from linear" spec for pulse-width-vs-angle response was retrieved; several promising primary sources (Hitec spec chart at hitecrcd.com, Hitec Analog vs Digital PDF at horizonhobby.com, a UT Austin general servo datasheet PDF, robotplatform.com's servo FAQ) were identified by search but could not be fetched in this session because their domains were blocked by the network egress proxy. ## Is backlash/gear slop direction-dependent, and how large is it typically for hobby vs. metal-gear/digital servos?

### Takeaway
Backlash in geared servos is inherently direction-dependent (the "reversal error" from approaching a target from opposite directions), is explicitly designed into the gear train to prevent binding, and is measurably smaller in metal-gear/higher-end digital or smart-serial servos (fractions of a degree to ~1° in arcminute-spec digital servos) than in plastic-gear analog/hobby servos (commonly 1–2°+, and reportedly growing with wear).

### Cited Findings
- "Backlash, sometimes called lash, play, or slop, refers to the clearance or lost motion in a mechanism... there will always be some degree of backlash in the gear train, because some clearance is essential so the gears and pinions don't bind together... most hobby servos have a fairly significant dead-band (where the shaft doesn't move with small changes in the control signal)" and backlash "may be a significant change... as a servo (or any other geared motor) wears" — [Pololu Forum: Gearbox/servo backlash documentation](https://forum.pololu.com/t/gearbox-servo-backlash-documentation/3799)
- "Reversal error is the distance between actual positions reached for a given target position when approached from the two opposite directions of travel, which is a combination of backlash and hysteresis" and "proportional actuator hysteresis is the direction-dependent position gap that appears when an axis reaches a different position at the same command after approaching from the opposite direction" — [Why Does Hysteresis Ruin Your Proportional Actuator, rodlesspneumatic.com](https://rodlesspneumatic.com/blog/why-does-hysteresis-ruin-your-proportional-actuator-precision-and-how-can-you-fix-it/)
- A dedicated open-source test stand for backlash measurement in "low-cost UART servo motors" is built specifically to do bidirectional measurement: it "eliminat[es] probe contact force by using the servo's own output-shaft encoder as the measurement device, applying small, repeatable loads to a servo lever and measuring resulting displacement under both loaded and unloaded conditions," comparing increasing vs. decreasing command approaches at identical commanded positions — confirming that direction-dependent bidirectional measurement is the accepted methodology for quantifying this in cheap serial/RC-style servos — [An open-source test stand for backlash measurement in low-cost UART servo motors, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2468067226000271) (full text not retrievable in this session, see Gaps)
- Manufacturer-published backlash specs for digital "smart" servos (Dynamixel line, direct from Robotis product pages): **MX-28AR ≈ 20 arcmin (≈0.33°)**, **MX-64AT ≈ 20 arcmin (≈0.33°)**, **XW430-T333-R ≈ 15 arcmin (≈0.25°)**, **XM540-W270-R ≈ 15 arcmin (≈0.25°)** — [Robotis product pages, MX-28AR / MX-64AT / XW430-T333-R / XM540-W270-R](https://www.robotis.us/dynamixel-mx-28ar/) (spec figures obtained via search-engine summary; direct fetch of robotis.us was blocked in this session)
- A hands-on test of Feetech "smart serial" servos found measured backlash of **STS3250: 0.278°**, **HLS3930M: 0.352°**, **STS3235: 0.439°** (STS3250 had the lowest of the group tested) — [Practical Comparison of Smart Serial Servo Motors for Robotics, robonine.com](https://robonine.com/smart-serial-servo-motor-comparison-robotics/) (via search summary; direct fetch blocked)
- Comparing plastic-gear vs metal-gear hobby micro servos: "the MG90S (metal gear) typically shows 1–2° less backlash than the SG90 (plastic gear)," and generally "metal gears can be machined to tighter tolerances than plastic, resulting in less backlash," while "as [plastic] gears develop backlash [with wear], a tiny gap forms between the teeth, making the servo less precise" — [MG90S vs SG90 comparison, Zbotic](https://zbotic.in/mg90s-vs-sg90-torque-accuracy-best-uses-compared/); [Metal Gear vs Plastic Gear Servo, RC Things](https://rcthing.com/metal-gear-vs-plastic-gear-servo/)
- Industrial-robot literature treats backlash explicitly as a bidirectional/direction-dependent phenomenon requiring separate modeling per approach direction: "Backlash errors are separated from angular positions by modeling bidirectional positioning errors with Chebyshev polynomials," with polynomial order (six, in one case) chosen by minimizing RMSE to avoid overfitting — a more rigorous/heavier analog of the same "approach from both directions and fit separately" logic used in cheap servo calibration — [A Modeling and Identification Method for Industrial Robot Positioning Accuracy Based on Parameter and Error Separation](https://doi.org/10.3390/machines14060678)
- A practical Arduino/hobby calibrator explicitly implements direction-averaging rather than treating backlash as negligible: it "sweeps the full range twice—once each direction—to construct the [20-point] table," averaging out the directional (hysteresis) component — [vishwam-aggarwal/Servo-Calibrator, GitHub](https://github.com/vishwam-aggarwal/Servo-Calibrator)

### Inferences
- Taken together, the numbers suggest a rough hierarchy: cheap plastic-gear analog hobby servos (SG90-class) sit at the high end, plausibly 1–2°+ of direction-dependent backlash (and worsening with wear); metal-gear hobby-grade digital servos (MG90S-class) improve on that by roughly 1–2°; and purpose-built "smart"/serial digital servos (Feetech STS/HLS, Dynamixel MX/X-series) are specified or measured in the sub-1° to ~0.3–0.4° range (15–20 arcmin ≈ 0.25–0.33° for Dynamixel MX/X-series). None of these eliminate direction dependence — they only shrink its magnitude — so a calibration routine that ignores approach direction will alias hysteresis into whatever function (linear, polynomial, or LUT) it fits, inflating apparent "nonlinearity."
- Because backlash/hysteresis is fundamentally a hidden-state (direction-dependent) effect rather than a static nonlinearity in pulse-width, no static curve-fit (linear, polynomial, or single-direction LUT) can fully capture it — the practitioner solution converged on in sources above is to sample/average (or separately fit) both approach directions rather than to add polynomial terms to a single-direction curve.

### Gaps
- Could not fetch full text of the "open-source test stand for backlash measurement in low-cost UART servo motors" paper (ScienceDirect, egress-blocked) or the STS3215-specific Robo9 test (also blocked) to get their headline backlash-in-degrees numbers for a plain low-cost RC/UART servo (only the smart-serial STS3250/HLS3930M/STS3235 comparison numbers were obtained, and those are for higher-end serial-bus servos, not plain 3-wire PWM hobby servos).
- No source gave a directly measured backlash figure (in degrees) for a plain analog 3-wire PWM hobby servo (e.g., a generic TowerPro/no-name analog servo) — the comparative numbers found are either for Dynamixel/Feetech smart-serial units or qualitative ("1-2° less" for MG90S vs SG90, without giving SG90's absolute figure).

## Do servos exhibit a "deadband" near center, and how is this normally handled in calibration code?

### Takeaway
Yes — deadband (a pulse-width range around the commanded position that produces no shaft motion) is a well-documented, named characteristic distinct from backlash; analog servos are commonly cited at roughly 5–8 µs of deadband and digital servos at roughly 1–3 µs (sometimes user-programmable from ~3–48 µs on Hitec digital units), and it is generally treated as a firmware/servo-internal property to be worked around (e.g., via minimum move increments or filtering) rather than something end-user PWM-generation code can shrink directly.

### Cited Findings
- "Deadband is a predefined range of error within which the servo's control circuit takes no corrective action, creating a 'zone of indifference' around the target position." Cited magnitudes: "Analog Servos: have a fixed, relatively wide deadband (often 5–10 microseconds of PWM pulse width, which can translate to a degree or more). Digital Servos: can have a much narrower deadband (as low as 1 µs) because the digital brain can filter noise intelligently." A specific example given: "The TowerPro SG90 micro servo has an internal deadband of approximately 7 µs, which translates to roughly 1–2 degrees of mechanical play." — [search summary; specifics attributed to microservomotor.com-style deadband explainer](https://microservomotor.com/common-specifications-and-parameters/servo-deadband-precision.htm)
- "Analog servos have a large 8 microsecond (8µs) deadband zone, while digital servos have a 1 microsecond (1µs) deadband zone — representing an 8-fold improvement in deadband performance for digital servos," attributed to digital servos' processors sending correction pulses at 300–400 Hz vs. ~50 Hz for analog, allowing much finer/faster micro-corrections (at the cost of higher standby current draw, ~50–100 mA+, from continuous micro-corrections) — [comparison summary sourced to Zbotic / Buildlog.net-style analog-vs-digital comparisons](https://www.buildlog.net/blog/2017/03/digital-vs-analog-servos/) (direct fetch blocked; via search synthesis)
- Hitec's own digital "Servo Programmer" tool allows deadband width to be changed from **3 µs to 48 µs**, confirming that deadband is an internally-set, adjustable firmware parameter on at least some digital servos rather than a fixed physical limit — [search summary referencing Hitec Servo Programmer documentation](https://www.rcuniverse.com/forum/rc-radios-transmitters-receivers-servos-gyros-157/1316167-deadband.html)
- General definition used by hobbyists: "if a servo has a deadband of 5µs and receives a command to center at 1.5ms, nothing happens until the pulse is greater than 1.5ms + 5µs" — i.e., deadband is framed as a symmetric no-motion window around the last commanded pulse width, not solely around the mechanical center — [Arduino Forum discussion "What is Dead Band Width?"](https://forum.arduino.cc/t/what-is-dead-band-width/379335) (via search summary)
- Dedicated practitioner writeups exist specifically titled "Servo Deadband Correction" (Instructables and jpralves.net), indicating deadband compensation is treated as its own named calibration step in hobbyist/maker practice distinct from linearity or backlash correction — [Servo Deadband Correction, Instructables](https://www.instructables.com/Servo-Deadband-Correction/); [Servo Deadband Correction, jpralves.net](https://jpralves.net/post/2020/12/24/servo-deadband-correction.html) (both pages egress-blocked in this session; titles/existence only, see Gaps)

### Inferences
- Because deadband is described as a window around the *last commanded* pulse width (not a fixed absolute zone at center), the practical calibration implication is that small incremental commands smaller than the deadband threshold (~1–8 µs depending on servo class) will be silently absorbed with no shaft movement — a calibration/control routine sweeping in fine steps should use a step size larger than the servo's deadband or it will see "flat" (non-moving) regions in its data that could be misread as some other nonlinearity.
- The digital-vs-analog deadband gap (roughly 1 µs vs ~5–8 µs) is on the same order as the unit-to-unit pulse-width variation reported elsewhere (±100 µs) is much larger — implying deadband alone is a second-order effect for coarse calibration but can matter for high-precision (sub-degree) closed-loop work, especially with analog servos.

### Gaps
- Could not retrieve the actual correction technique/code described in the two dedicated "Servo Deadband Correction" writeups (Instructables, jpralves.net) — both domains were blocked by the network egress proxy in this session. Their existence and titles are confirmed but the specific compensation algorithm (e.g., dithering, minimum-step-size enforcement, or hysteresis-band widening in software) could not be extracted.
- No primary manufacturer datasheet with an explicit deadband spec (in µs) was directly fetched; all deadband figures above come from secondary comparison/explainer sources reached only via search-engine summaries, so they should be treated as generally-consistent community consensus rather than confirmed manufacturer-published numbers.

## What modeling approaches do practitioners actually use (linear, piecewise-linear, polynomial, spline/LUT), and what are the tradeoffs?

### Takeaway
In practice, hobbyist/maker code overwhelmingly defaults to a single global linear (2-point) fit or the Arduino `map()` function because it is simple and "good enough" for typical ±1° tolerances; when higher accuracy is needed, the next step used in published work is a multi-point (piecewise-linear-by-interpolation) lookup table built from directly-measured PWM/angle pairs (sometimes direction-averaged to also cancel hysteresis), while full polynomial curve-fitting and Chebyshev-polynomial bidirectional models appear mainly in the industrial-robot backlash-compensation literature rather than in RC-servo hobbyist code.

### Cited Findings
- Simple/global linear approach: "You can easily calculate the pulse width required for any servo angle using the line of best fit... which can be obtained in Excel. Linear regression can be used on the data set to work out what formula relates angle and PWM value," and a common code shortcut is "the Arduino map() function... allowing you to map degrees to PWM values directly" — [servo calibration search synthesis, Hackster.io/Medium write-ups](https://www.hackster.io/ian-hong/exploring-servo-motor-calibration-414b95)
- 2-point/no-full-calibration approach: for a servo already known to be accurate/linear (e.g. DS3235), "you may not need a full calibration if you know the pulse width difference between two known positions" — [Calibrating My Servos, Hackster.io](https://www.hackster.io/jeremy-lindsay/calibrating-my-servos-fa27ce)
- Manual multi-point lookup-table approach: "record PWM and angle value pairs in an array in an Arduino sketch for reference when moving the servo," built by "rotat[ing] a potentiometer until the servo needle aligns with angle marks on a protractor and not[ing] the PWM values, repeating this process every 10 degrees until reaching 180 degrees" — [servo calibration search synthesis](https://www.hackster.io/ian-hong/exploring-servo-motor-calibration-414b95)
- Automated, sensor-verified lookup-table approach: an open-source tool builds a "**20-point direction-averaged lookup table**" using an AS5600 magnetic encoder as ground truth, via a "coarse-then-fine scan outward from center in both directions to find mechanical limits, then sweeps the full range twice—once each direction—to construct the table." This reduced mean positioning error from 3.13° (linear formula) to 0.19° on one unit, and gave a 2–6× error reduction across two servos tested, at a storage cost of "approximately 80 bytes" for the table — [vishwam-aggarwal/Servo-Calibrator, GitHub](https://github.com/vishwam-aggarwal/Servo-Calibrator)
- Polynomial/Chebyshev modeling for bidirectional (backlash-inclusive) error: in industrial robot joint calibration, "backlash errors are separated from angular positions by modeling their bidirectional positioning errors with Chebyshev polynomials," with polynomial order chosen (six, in one paper) "based on root mean square error minimization to avoid overfitting" — a heavier statistical approach than hobby practice, aimed at combining geometric + backlash error correction for precision industrial arms rather than simple RC servos — [A Modeling and Identification Method for Industrial Robot Positioning Accuracy Based on Parameter and Error Separation](https://doi.org/10.3390/machines14060678)
- A related industrial technique uses a hybrid affine + low-order polynomial correction: "an affine correction for static attitude-related errors and a low-order polynomial term to approximate residual nonlinear errors," for mobile servo gimbal pointing-error correction — illustrating that when full polynomials are used, practitioners still keep the order low and pair it with a simpler affine/linear base term rather than replacing the linear term outright — [Pointing-error correction model for mobile servo gimbals, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1270963826010813) (abstract-level; full text egress-blocked)

### Inferences
- There is a clear practical hierarchy of effort vs. accuracy in the sources: (1) single linear fit / `map()` — near-zero effort, ~few-degree error; (2) 2-point calibration using measured endpoints instead of nominal 1000/2000 µs — corrects unit-to-unit offset/scale but not curvature or hysteresis; (3) multi-point (5–20+ point) lookup table with interpolation — captures curvature and, if swept both directions, also averages out hysteresis, at the cost of needing a measurement rig (protractor+eye, or an angle encoder) and modest memory (tens of bytes to a few hundred); (4) polynomial/Chebyshev bidirectional models — used where the underlying physical accuracy requirement is much tighter than RC-servo use cases justify (industrial robot arms), and even there people prefer low polynomial order plus a linear/affine base rather than a single high-order global polynomial, because of overfitting risk.
- No source in this search actually recommends full high-order single-polynomial fitting for consumer RC servos; the "upgrade path" documented in the wild goes straight from linear to lookup-table/piecewise-interpolation, not to polynomial. This is likely because a LUT with interpolation is simpler to build from raw calibration-sweep data (no curve-fitting math required) and naturally handles a servo whose curvature shape isn't well-described by a low-order polynomial (e.g., has a flat deadband region plus a curved end-of-travel region).

### Gaps
- Could not find a hobbyist/RC-servo-specific project that explicitly chose and justified a global polynomial fit (as opposed to a LUT) — this modeling choice appears concentrated in industrial-robot and gimbal-pointing literature, not RC servo calibration write-ups; if the requester specifically needs a polynomial-fit precedent for RC servos, none was found and this should be treated as a likely gap in publicly documented practice, not a confirmed absence.

## Under what conditions is a simple single linear fit per servo considered "good enough," vs. when do people upgrade to something more complex?

### Takeaway
A single per-unit linear fit (measured endpoints/slope for that specific servo, not the nominal 1000/1500/2000 µs assumption) is repeatedly treated as adequate when the required precision is "to the nearest degree" or coarser and the servo in question is a known-good/linear model; practitioners move to multi-point lookup tables (with direction-averaging) specifically when they need sub-degree accuracy, are working with a servo that is not known to be well-behaved, or need to null out hysteresis/backlash rather than just curvature.

### Cited Findings
- "Accuracy to the nearest degree is good enough for servo calibration purposes" is presented as a workable target achievable via linear regression — implying that when ~1° tolerance suffices, the linear approach is treated as sufficient by at least some practitioners — [servo calibration search synthesis](https://www.hackster.io/ian-hong/exploring-servo-motor-calibration-414b95)
- Explicit "skip full calibration" case: for a servo independently known to be accurate/linear (DS3235 cited by name), knowing only the pulse-width difference between two known positions (i.e., a 2-point linear model) is described as sufficient — [Calibrating My Servos, Hackster.io](https://www.hackster.io/jeremy-lindsay/calibrating-my-servos-fa27ce)
- Explicit case for upgrading: the same author/toolchain that built the AS5600-based 20-point-table calibrator reports the linear-formula error (3.13° mean) was large enough, relative to their target application's tolerance, to motivate building direction-averaged multi-point calibration instead, cutting error 2–6× — [vishwam-aggarwal/Servo-Calibrator, GitHub](https://github.com/vishwam-aggarwal/Servo-Calibrator)
- The variation in behavior "by model of servo" and "even between units of the same model" is the reason given for calibrating "if you want to maximize accuracy" at all, i.e., the decision to calibrate (linear or otherwise) is driven by whether the application needs to maximize accuracy versus tolerating stock/nominal behavior — [servo calibration search synthesis](https://www.hackster.io/ian-hong/exploring-servo-motor-calibration-414b95)

### Inferences
- Combining this with the magnitude data above: a single global linear fit (even using measured, not nominal, endpoints) appears adequate when (a) the target tolerance is roughly ≥1°, (b) the specific servo/model is independently known or verified to track linearly (e.g., certain higher-end analog or well-regarded digital models), and (c) the application does not require canceling out direction-dependent backlash (e.g., single-direction moves, or backlash is smaller than the tolerance). A move to a denser (multi-point/LUT, direction-averaged) model is warranted when target tolerance drops toward or below ~0.5–1°, when the servo is unverified/cheap/plastic-gear (where multi-degree combined nonlinearity+backlash is plausible per the 3.13°-vs-0.19° finding), or when the use case requires repeatable bidirectional positioning (approaching the same commanded angle from both directions with consistent results).

### Gaps
- No source gave a formal decision rule or published threshold (e.g., "if backlash/nonlinearity exceeds X°, use a LUT") — the "good enough" judgment in every source found is qualitative/anecdotal (per-project tolerance), not derived from a general engineering guideline or manufacturer recommendation.
- Could not confirm whether any standards body or manufacturer publishes a formal linearity tolerance spec that would let a practitioner decide up front (before testing) whether a given servo model needs more than a linear fit; this remains an open question given the general difficulty accessing manufacturer datasheet PDFs in this session (see recurring egress-block notes above).
